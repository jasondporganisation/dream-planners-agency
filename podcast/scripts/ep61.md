## Script

Here's a slightly awkward truth about our profession. We spend our days helping other people think about their money. And then some of us go home and quietly worry about our own.

Not because we don't know better. But because of how our income arrives. Some months are strong. Some months are thin. And when the money in your account goes up and down like that, even ordinary bills can start to feel stressful. Rent. Groceries. School fees. The same bills every month, paid out of income that is anything but the same.

So today, let's talk about that. Not the theory. The habits.

Before we start, a quick note. This episode is about personal habits. It's general information. It's not advice on any financial product, and it's not tax advice. Your own situation is your own, so check the details for yourself, or speak to a tax professional where you need to.

I'm Jason Ng, and this is Quiet Compounding.

[pause]

We're in our second theme, the working week and your energy. And money belongs in this theme. Because money worry is one of the quietest drains on your energy there is. It follows you into meetings. It sits with you at night. And it can push you towards decisions you wouldn't make with a clear head.

So here's the one idea for today. Pay yourself a salary. Let your income land in one place, and then move the same fixed amount to your spending money every month, whatever happened that month. Good months fill the buffer. Thin months draw on it. And your everyday life stops swinging with your commissions.

[pause]

Let's start with the fact that you're not alone in this.

Singapore's Ministry of Manpower publishes a yearly labour force report. In its twenty twenty-five findings, there were one hundred and seventy-nine thousand, two hundred regular primary own account workers. That's people who work for themselves, as their main job. They made up seven point three per cent of employed residents.

And when the report looked at the challenges those workers faced, the top ones were about work itself. Uncertainty of finding enough work, at nine per cent. And not having enough work, at seven point one per cent.

I want to be careful here. Those figures cover all own account workers, not advisers specifically. But the point stands. Uneven income is a normal part of working for yourself. It's not a sign you're doing something wrong. It's just the shape of the work. And it deserves a system.

[pause]

So what does the system look like? Let's take it in four parts.

Part one. Budget on what you can count on.

MoneySense, Singapore's national financial education programme, has a line I find really helpful for people like us. When you budget, include the income you can count on, and, I'll quote it, "do not include any money you are not sure you will earn", and it gives the example of bonuses and commissions.

Think about how unusual that advice sounds for someone whose income is mostly commission. But that's exactly the point. If you build your monthly life around your best months, every ordinary month will feel like a shortfall. If you build it around what you can reliably cover, a good month becomes a gift to your buffer, not a reason to spend more.

[pause]

Part two. Build a cash buffer first.

MAS's Basic Financial Planning Guide, launched in October twenty twenty-three, says to set aside at least three to six months of expenses for emergency needs. MoneySense says something similar, three to six times your monthly expenses as emergency funds.

And there's a detail in MAS's consumer FAQ on that guide that speaks right to us. It says the right amount differs from person to person. And it says that a freelancer with unpredictable income may want to set aside a higher amount, to cover lull periods.

You may have heard a specific bigger number quoted as an official rule for people on commission. I couldn't find that on the official pages I checked, so I won't give you one. What I can say is that MAS has said people with unpredictable income may want more than the usual three to six months. How much more is a personal decision.

I'm also not going to tell you where to keep that buffer. That's not what this episode is about. What matters here is the habit. Having one, and topping it up.

[pause]

Part three. Pay yourself a steady amount.

This is the heart of it. Let all your commission land in one place. Then, on the same date every month, move a fixed amount to the place you spend from. That's your salary. Same amount, every month.

Here's why I like it. It separates two questions that get tangled together. "How did my business do this month?" and "Can I pay my bills?" With a salary, the second question has the same answer every month. The first question still matters. But it's no longer keeping you up at night.

[pause]

Part four. Set money aside for tax as it comes in.

As a self-employed person, the tax on your income is worked out after you file your return. Which means the bill arrives months after you earned the money. If you've spent it all by then, that bill can hurt.

So put a share aside as the income comes in. How much depends on your own situation, so I won't give you a percentage. Check your own position, or speak to a tax professional.

And when the bill does arrive, IRAS lets you pay individual income tax by GIRO in monthly instalments, up to twelve months. The plan runs from May to April, with deductions on the sixth of each month. That can make the bill feel a lot more manageable.

And don't forget MediSave. If you're self-employed and your net trade income is above a certain level, you'll have MediSave contributions to make too. We'll look at that properly in two episodes' time.

[pause]

Let me give you a picture.

Picture an adviser whose income comes in waves. In a strong month, he used to upgrade things. A new phone, a nicer dinner, a weekend away. In a quiet month, he'd lie awake worrying about rent. His lifestyle was moving with his commissions, up and down, and so was his mood.

So he changed the plumbing. Now all his commission goes into one income account. On the first of every month, he moves the same fixed amount to his spending account. He puts a set share aside for tax and MediSave. And whatever's left stays where it is, as his buffer.

Quiet months are still quiet. That hasn't changed. But they no longer feel like a crisis. Because his salary still arrives on the first. And when a strong month comes, it doesn't change his life. It just makes his buffer a little thicker.

There's another benefit he didn't expect. He feels calmer in client meetings. There's no money worry sitting at the back of his mind, nudging him to rush. He can just focus on the person in front of him.

[pause]

So here are three things to do this week.

First, list your fixed monthly expenses and add them up. Rent or mortgage. Bills. Food. Transport. Family commitments. That number is your steady pay target.

Second, set up one standing instruction. On the same date each month, move that amount from where your income lands to where you spend.

Third, book a monthly money hour in your calendar. You can tie it to your weekly review from two episodes ago, on the first week of each month. In that hour, check your buffer, check what you've put aside for tax and MediSave, and adjust your steady pay if your fixed costs have changed. Small, boring habits beat big resolutions.

[pause]

Here's what I'd like you to carry this week. You can't make every month the same. But you can make your life feel the same, every month. That's what a salary to yourself does.

Next time, we turn from money to the mind. Specifically, to comparison. That colleague's post. That award photo. And what the highlight reel does to how you feel about your own work.

Thanks for listening to Quiet Compounding. These are my personal views as a representative, and I'm not speaking on behalf of any company. It's general discussion, not advice for your situation, and not tax advice. Take care, and I'll see you next time.
