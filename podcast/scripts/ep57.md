## Script

Think back to yesterday. Not the plan. What actually happened.

Maybe it went something like this. A meeting in the morning. Then twenty messages waiting when you came out. You answered some. You started a follow-up note, then a call came in. You took the call. You came back to the note, but you'd lost the thread. Then it was lunch. Then another meeting. And at the end of the day, you looked at your list and the one thing that really mattered, the thing you promised yourself you'd do, was still sitting there.

If that sounds familiar, you're not lazy. Your week just doesn't have a shape yet.

I'm Jason Ng, and this is Quiet Compounding.

[pause]

Last week we started a new theme, the working week and your energy. We began with burnout, and how to spot the early signs. Today we're going to be practical. Because one of the quiet drivers of that tired, scattered feeling is a week with no structure. Everything competing with everything, all day long.

So here's the one idea for today. Give every kind of work a home in your week. Client meetings have a home. Prospecting and follow-ups have a home. Admin and notes have a home. Learning has a home. And rest has a home too. That's what people mean by time-blocking. It's not a fancy system. It's just deciding, ahead of time, when each type of work gets done.

Let me explain why it helps, using a bit of research. And I'll be careful here. These are studies done in labs and research settings. They weren't studies of financial advisers. So I'll tell you what they found, and you can decide how it fits your week.

[pause]

The first idea is that switching has a cost.

In two thousand and one, a group of researchers ran four lab experiments where people either repeated the same task or kept alternating between different tasks. When people alternated, they were slower. That's what the researchers called switching-time costs. And those costs grew when the rules of the tasks were more complex. They shrank when people had clear cues about which task to do next.

Now, you'll see big percentages floating around online about how much multitasking costs you. I'm not going to quote any of them, because I couldn't find them checked on a proper source. What I can say is simpler. In those experiments, jumping between tasks took extra time, and harder tasks made it worse.

Think about our work. A client review needs one kind of thinking. A prospecting call needs another. Writing up meeting notes properly needs another again. When you bounce between them every few minutes, each jump costs you something.

[pause]

The second idea is about what unfinished work does to you.

A researcher named Sophie Leroy published a study in two thousand and nine. In two experiments, she found that people struggled to shift their attention away from a task they hadn't finished. And their performance on the next task suffered. She called this attention residue. Part of your mind stays behind with the thing you left.

There's a detail in that study I find useful. Just finishing the first task wasn't enough on its own. Finishing it under some time pressure helped people let it go and do better on the next task.

So what does that mean for an adviser? Two things. Group similar work together, so you're not dragging residue from one kind of task into a very different one. And when you have to stop something halfway, close it properly. Write one line: where I stopped, and what's next. That little note gives your mind permission to let go.

[pause]

The third idea is about making each block specific.

There's a body of research on what are called implementation intentions. That's a fancy name for an if-then plan. "If it's Tuesday at nine, then I make follow-up calls from my list." In two thousand and six, two researchers, Gollwitzer and Sheeran, pulled together ninety-four independent tests of these plans. They found the plans had a medium-to-large positive effect on reaching goals.

I like this because it's so plain. A block in your calendar that just says "prospecting" is easy to ignore. A block that says "If it's Tuesday at nine, then I call the five people on my follow-up list" is much harder to drift away from. You've already made the decision. All that's left is to do it.

[pause]

Let me give you a picture of how this looks in a real week.

Picture an adviser whose day is a string of chat replies, half-written notes, and calls squeezed in between meetings. He's busy all day. But he's not getting the important things done.

So he sits down on a Friday and gives his week a shape. Monday and Thursday mornings are for follow-up calls. Tuesday and Wednesday afternoons are for client meetings. And there's a short slot at five o'clock every day, just to finish that day's meeting notes. Inside each block, he writes one if-then line, so he knows exactly what he's doing when he gets there.

He also leaves some space empty on purpose. Because clients don't always fit his plan, and he knows that.

When a meeting runs over, he doesn't just rush into the next thing. He takes thirty seconds and writes a "where I stopped" note. Then he moves on.

Nothing about his workload changed. But after a few weeks, fewer things are left half-done. And he goes home with less of the day still rattling around in his head.

[pause]

Now, a few honest cautions.

First, time-blocking is not about ignoring clients. If a client has an urgent need during your prospecting block, you don't pretend you didn't see it. What helps is deciding in advance how you'll handle urgent things. Maybe you check messages at set points between blocks. Maybe you tell clients when you're usually reachable. You decide what's right for your practice, and your clients still come first.

Second, leave buffer time. A calendar packed edge to edge breaks the first time a meeting overruns. A bit of empty space is not wasted time. It's what keeps the rest of the plan standing.

Third, and this matters, put your compliance tasks in the calendar too. Writing up records of advice. Updating files. These aren't things to push to "later in the week" and hope for the best. Timing rules for records are something to check with your compliance team, and then build into your blocks so they actually happen.

And fourth, this isn't the only way to plan a week. It's one approach that many people find calming. If you try it and adjust it, that's the point. Your week, your shape.

[pause]

So here are three things to do this week.

First, open next week's calendar and book three blocks. One for prospecting or follow-ups. One for admin and notes. And one with nothing in it at all. That last one is your buffer. Protect it.

Second, inside each block, write one if-then line. "If it's Thursday at nine, then I call the people on my follow-up list." "If it's five o'clock, then I finish today's meeting notes." Be specific enough that future you doesn't have to decide anything.

Third, practise closing tasks. Whenever you have to stop something halfway this week, write one line before you move on. Where I stopped, and what's next. See how it feels to walk into the next thing with a clearer head.

[pause]

Here's what I'd like you to remember. A busy week and a productive week are not the same thing. Most of us don't need more hours. We need the hours we have to stop fighting each other.

None of this needs a new app or a weekend workshop. It needs one Friday afternoon, a calendar, and a bit of honesty about how your week really goes. Start small. Three blocks. Then see what changes.

Give every kind of work a home, and your week gets a little quieter.

Next time, we'll go deeper on something that sits underneath all of this. How habits actually form. Why good routines fall apart after a week, and what the research says about making them stick.

Thanks for listening to Quiet Compounding. These are my personal views as a representative, and I'm not speaking on behalf of any company. It's general discussion, not advice for your situation. This episode was checked by my team. Take care, and I'll see you next time.
