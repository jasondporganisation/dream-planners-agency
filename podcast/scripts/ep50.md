## Script

It's ten in the morning. You send a message to someone you've been looking forward to meeting. They reply. "Thanks, but not right now."

It's polite. It's short. It's completely reasonable.

And somehow it sits with you for the rest of the day. You replay it at lunch. You hesitate before the next message. By evening, one small "no" has turned into a quiet question about whether you're cut out for this at all.

If that's happened to you, you're not weak, and you're not alone in feeling it. Let's talk about why it happens, and what to do about it.

[pause]

I'm Jason Ng, and this is Quiet Compounding. This is a show for financial advisory professionals in Singapore about building the practice, and the person behind it. Everything here is my personal view as a representative, I'm not speaking on behalf of any company, and it's general.

This is a Resilient Advisor episode. So today isn't about systems or scripts. It's about you. Specifically, the question I hear in different forms all the time. Why does one "no" ruin my whole day, and how do I stop it piling up?

Here's the idea I want to build on. A "no" is an event. What often hurts more is the story we attach to it. And you can learn to handle both, kindly.

Let's start with the sting itself.

[pause]

Rejection hurts, and there's some research on why. In October two thousand and three, a team of researchers published a study in the journal Science. They scanned people's brains while they played a simple virtual ball-tossing game. At some point, the other players stopped throwing the ball to them. They were left out.

During that exclusion, a part of the brain called the anterior cingulate cortex was more active than when they were included. And the more active it was, the more distress people reported. The researchers noted this paralleled what had been seen in studies of physical pain.

Now, I want to be careful here, because this kind of finding gets stretched. It does not mean a "no" is the same as a broken bone. What the research suggests is some overlap in the brain regions involved, in a lab task. That's all.

And the science is still being refined. In twenty fifteen, a review pulled together forty-six brain-imaging articles on this, covering nine hundred and forty participants, mostly using that same ball-tossing game. It found activity linked to social pain in more than one part of that brain region, and asked which parts are really involved. So the picture is still being worked out.

But here's what I take from it. If a "no" stings, that's normal. It's not a sign you're in the wrong job. It's a sign you're a person who cares about connection. Which, in this work, is a good thing to be.

[pause]

So the sting is real. What turns a sting into a ruined day is usually what comes next. The story.

Let's separate the two.

The event is this: "They said no to a meeting." That's it. That's the whole fact.

The story is everything we add. "I'm bad at this." "They didn't like me." "Everyone can tell I'm struggling." "I should give up."

None of those are in the message. We wrote them. And we tend to write them fast, without noticing.

So a really useful skill to build is catching the gap between the event and the story. Just noticing it helps. "Okay. The fact is they said not now. The rest is something I'm telling myself."

It also helps to remember what a "no" usually means on the other side. "Not now" can mean they're busy this month. It can mean they've just had a baby, or changed jobs, or simply don't want a meeting this week. You don't know. And because you don't know, the story that it's all about you is just a guess. Usually an unkind one.

Try this the next time it happens. Say the event out loud, in one sentence, the way a neutral observer would describe it. No adjectives. No conclusions. You'll often find the sentence is much smaller than the feeling. That doesn't make the feeling wrong. It just puts it back to its real size.

[pause]

Now, when we catch ourselves spiralling, it's tempting to reach for toughness. Snap out of it. Push through. Be harder on yourself so it doesn't happen again.

There's research that points another way.

In twenty twelve, two researchers published a set of four experiments in the Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin. They compared people who were encouraged to treat a setback with self-compassion, against people who were encouraged to boost their self-esteem, and people given no special instruction.

The self-compassion group came out ahead on motivation. After an initial test failure, they spent more time studying for the next test. They reported more motivation to change a personal weakness. And after recalling a moral slip, they reported more motivation to make amends.

These were lab experiments, not studies of advisers. But the point is useful. Being kind to yourself after a setback is not letting yourself off. In those experiments, it went with wanting to improve more, not less.

Self-compassion here simply means talking to yourself the way you'd talk to a new colleague who'd just had a rough morning. Honest. Warm. Practical.

[pause]

Let me put this together into something you can actually do.

Picture an adviser who gets three "not now" replies before lunch. By the third one, he's half-drafting a message to his manager about quitting.

But he's practised a short reset, so he tries it instead.

He takes out his phone and writes one plain line about what happened. "Three people said not now this morning." Then he writes the story he's telling himself. "I'm not good enough for this." Then he writes what he'd say to a new colleague in the same spot. Something like, "That's a tough morning. It's three replies, not a verdict on you."

Then he puts his phone down and walks round the block.

When he gets back, he sends a short, warm reply to each person. "Thanks for letting me know. If anything changes, I'm here." No chasing. No second push. Just a clean, kind exit that leaves the door open.

And then he does one admin task he's been avoiding for a week. Something small and useful.

Is it a great day? No. But it isn't ruined either. And that's the goal. Not to feel nothing. Just to stop one hard moment from taking the whole day.

[pause]

I want to underline one part of that story. The reply.

Respecting a "no" matters, for them and for you. A warm, clean exit keeps the relationship intact. And it keeps you well away from pressure. If you notice yourself wanting to ask again and again until someone gives in, that's the sting talking, not good practice. Let the "no" be a "no".

And if you're coming to this from last episode, on building referrals into your process, this is the other side of it. When you ask more often, you'll hear more noes. So it's worth having a way to handle them.

[pause]

So here are three things to try this week.

First, write a three-line "after a no" card. Line one: what happened, in plain facts. Line two: what I'd tell a friend in the same spot. Line three: one small next task. Keep it on your phone.

Second, the next time a "no" lands hard, actually use the card. Then take a short break. A walk, a glass of water, a few minutes away from the screen. Then do the small task on line three.

Third, write yourself one warm, simple reply you can send when someone says no. Something like, "Thanks for letting me know. If anything changes, I'm here." Use it, and then let it go.

[pause]

One more thing, and it's important.

Everything today is about the ordinary sting of an ordinary "no". But if your low mood is lasting, or it feels heavy, please talk to someone. A friend, a family member, a doctor. In Singapore, Samaritans of Singapore has a twenty-four-hour hotline on one seven six seven, and a twenty-four-hour CareText messaging service at nine one five one, one seven six seven. And if you or someone else is in immediate danger, call nine nine five or nine nine nine.

Reaching out is not a weakness. It's part of looking after the person behind the practice.

[pause]

Here's the line I'd like you to take with you.

A "no" is something that happened to you today, not something that you are.

Thanks for listening to Quiet Compounding. I'm Jason Ng. Be kind to yourself this week, and I'll see you next time.
