## Script

It's Friday afternoon. The week is nearly over. And there's a pile waiting for you. Forms to complete. Notes to write up. Files to submit. You open the first one. It's from a meeting on Tuesday. And you sit there, trying to remember. Why did I suggest that option and not the other one? What exactly did the client say about their plans? Was it their daughter's school fees, or their son's?

You get through it. You always do. But it doesn't feel great. And somewhere in the back of your mind, there's a small worry. Is this really how it's supposed to work?

I'm Jason Ng, and this is Quiet Compounding.

[pause]

Today we start a new theme. Compliance, ethics, and hard conversations. Over the next few episodes, we'll talk about pressure, social media, difficult conversations with clients, complaints, and speaking up when something feels wrong.

And I want to start with something that might sound dull. But I think it quietly holds everything else up.

Here's the one idea. Compliance isn't a once-a-year audit, and it isn't a Friday afternoon pile. It's a set of small checks you do every working day, as part of the work itself. The fact-find. The reasons for your recommendation. The disclosures. The records. Done in the room, or the same day, while it's fresh.

[pause]

Before I go on, one important note. Nothing I say today is a compliance sign-off. I'm not your compliance team, and I can't tell you what's required in your firm. Every firm has its own policies, its own forms and its own processes. So please treat this as a way of thinking about the habit, and check the details with your compliance team.

[pause]

Let's start with the big picture, from MAS.

MAS has Guidelines on Fair Dealing. The current version took effect on the thirtieth of May, twenty twenty-four. It's reference F S G dash G zero four, and it replaced the earlier guidelines.

These guidelines are written for boards and senior management of financial institutions. So they're not addressed to you and me directly. But MAS says they apply across all financial products and services, and that the principles should be applied to all customers. And they set out five outcomes. Let me read them, because I think they're worth hearing.

One. Customers have confidence that they deal with financial institutions where fair dealing is central to the corporate culture.

Two. Financial institutions offer products and services that are suitable for their target customer segments.

Three. Customers are served by competent representatives.

Four. Customers receive clear, relevant and timely information.

And five. Financial institutions handle complaints in an independent, effective and prompt manner.

Now, notice something. Even though these are written for boards, almost every one of them shows up in a normal client meeting. Outcome three is about us directly. Competent representatives. Outcome four is about how clearly we explain things. Outcome five, we'll come back to in a later episode on complaints.

[pause]

So what does "competent" look like on a Tuesday morning, in a meeting room?

The guidelines give us a clue. They expect firms to train representatives to make reasonable enquiries, to collect enough information to understand the customer's needs and objectives, and, I'll quote this part, to "fully document the basis of recommendation".

That phrase is the heart of today's episode. The basis of recommendation. In plain words: why did you suggest what you suggested, for this person, given what they told you about their goals, their situation and their needs?

If you can explain that clearly, in writing, in a way someone else could follow, you've done something important. For the client, and for yourself.

And here's the problem with Friday afternoons. By Friday, the reasons are fuzzy. You remember the outcome, but not the thinking. And a basis of recommendation written from a fuzzy memory is not the same as one written while the conversation is still in your head.

[pause]

There's one more piece of the picture worth knowing about.

MAS has a notice called F A A dash N twenty, which sets requirements for how representatives and supervisors are paid and assessed. It's known as the Balanced Scorecard Framework. There are also guidelines that go with it, F A A dash G fourteen. Both were last revised on the twenty-ninth of December, twenty twenty-five.

I'm not going to describe the grades, or what happens at each one, because that's not something I can get into properly here. Your firm will have its own briefing on how it works, and that's the right place to learn the details. But here's the simple point for today. The framework looks at non-sales factors as well as sales. And the way you work every day, your fact-finds, your documentation, your disclosures, all feeds into that record over time.

So the daily habit isn't just good practice. It's part of how your work is seen.

[pause]

So how do you build compliance into the meeting, rather than after it?

Here are a few ideas that I find helpful.

Use your firm's checklist in the room. Not afterwards. Let it guide the conversation, so you're not trying to fill gaps later.

Write the basis of recommendation while it's fresh. Ideally before you leave the meeting, or at least the same day. In plain words. The client's goals as they described them. And your reasons.

File the same day. Don't let it build into a pile.

And when you're unsure, ask early. Your compliance team would much rather hear a question before something is submitted than have to fix a problem after.

[pause]

Let me give you a picture of how this can look.

Picture an adviser who used to do all her compliance work on Friday afternoons. It was a habit. Meetings during the week, paperwork at the end. But by Friday, she often couldn't remember why she'd recommended one option over another on Tuesday. She'd have to piece it together from scribbled notes. It took hours, and it didn't feel solid.

So she changed one thing. Before leaving each meeting room, she spends ten minutes writing down the client's stated goals, and her reasons, in plain words, on her firm's form.

Just ten minutes. And it changed more than she expected.

Her Fridays got quieter. The pile disappeared. And a few months later, when her supervisor asked her about one particular case, she didn't have to guess. She could answer from the file, not from memory. Everything was there, in her own words, written on the day.

That's what the habit buys you. Not just tidiness. Confidence.

And there's a quieter benefit for the client too. When she writes down their goals while they're still in the room, she sometimes reads them back. "So what I've heard is this. Have I got that right?" More than once, a client has corrected her. That correction, caught in the room, is exactly the kind of thing a Friday afternoon would have missed.

[pause]

Let me also be honest about two traps.

The first is thinking compliance is just a box-ticking exercise. It isn't. The boxes are there to make sure the thinking happened. If you tick the box without the thinking, you've missed the point, and you've left yourself exposed.

The second is thinking that as long as the client signs, you're covered. A signature isn't a substitute for a proper fact-find, a clear explanation, and a documented reason. Check with your compliance team on what your firm expects.

[pause]

So here are three things to do this week.

First, pick one client meeting this week. Just one. Finish your basis of recommendation notes the same day, before you go home, using your firm's template.

Second, notice how long it took, and what you'd probably have forgotten by Friday. Write that down. It's useful to see.

Third, write down one compliance question you've been putting off, and send it to your compliance team this week. Ask early.

[pause]

Here's what I'd like you to carry this week. Compliance done in the room is lighter than compliance done on Friday. And it's a lot more honest, because it's written while you still remember why.

Next time, we'll look at something harder. What happens to our ethics when targets and pressure build up. And how to stay steady when you feel the pull.

Thanks for listening to Quiet Compounding. These are my personal views as a representative, and I'm not speaking on behalf of any company. It's general discussion, not compliance or legal advice, and not advice for your situation. Please check with your compliance team. This episode was checked by my team. Take care, and I'll see you next time.
