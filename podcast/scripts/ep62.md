## Script

It's late. You've had a decent day, actually. A good conversation with a client. A follow-up that went well. Nothing dramatic, but solid.

And then you pick up your phone. A colleague on a stage, holding an award. Another one at an overseas conference, smiling by a pool. A team photo with a caption about another record month. You keep scrolling. And by the time you put the phone down, that decent day has turned into something else. A quiet, heavy feeling that you're behind. That everyone else is doing better. That maybe you're failing.

Nothing about your day changed. Only what you compared it with.

I'm Jason Ng, and this is Quiet Compounding.

[pause]

Before we go further, a gentle note. If low mood is something that lasts for you, more than a passing feeling after scrolling, please talk to a doctor. And if you need someone to talk to now, you can call National Mindline on one seven seven one, or the Samaritans of Singapore on one seven six seven. Both are open twenty-four hours.

[pause]

We're in our second theme, the working week and your energy. Last week, we talked about money habits. Today we talk about something that can drain you just as quietly. Comparison.

Here's the one idea I want you to take away. You can't stop comparing. It's human. But you can choose a better yardstick.

[pause]

Let's start with why comparison is so natural.

Back in nineteen fifty-four, a psychologist called Leon Festinger put forward what became known as social comparison theory. His idea was that people are driven to work out how they're doing, how good their opinions and abilities are. And when there's no objective way to measure that, we compare ourselves with other people. He also suggested we tend to pick people who are similar to us.

Now think about our work. How do you know if you're doing well as an adviser? It's not like running a race, with a clock at the finish line. There are very few clear, objective yardsticks for "a good month" in this job. So what do we do? We look sideways. At the people most like us. Other advisers.

That's why those posts land so hard. They're not from strangers. They're from the very people your mind has picked as the measuring stick.

[pause]

And then there's the nature of the feed itself.

A feed is a highlight reel. People post the stage, not the rejections before it. The conference, not the quiet weeks. The record month, not the months that weren't. That's not dishonest. It's just what people share.

But it means you're comparing your full, behind-the-scenes week, every doubt, every no, every slow afternoon, with someone else's best moments. That's not a fair test. Nobody wins that comparison.

I also want to say this clearly. I'm not going to suggest that anyone posting is faking it. We don't know other people's stories, and it's not fair to guess. The point isn't about them. It's about what the comparison does to you.

[pause]

Now, what does the research say about social media and how we feel? I want to be careful here, because this is an area where the headlines often go further than the evidence.

In twenty fifteen, Philippe Verduyn and colleagues published two studies, one in a lab and one tracking people in daily life, looking at one social networking site. They found that passive use, meaning just scrolling and looking without interacting, led to drops in how good people felt over time. And in the daily-life study, that was linked to increased envy.

A review in twenty seventeen, by Verduyn and others, pulled together more research. It found passive use was negatively linked to wellbeing, through social comparison and envy. Active use, like messaging and interacting, was linked to better wellbeing, though that link was less robust.

So far, that sounds like a neat rule. Passive scrolling bad. Active use good.

But here's the important part. In twenty twenty-two, Patti Valkenburg and colleagues looked at forty survey-based studies. And they found that most of those studies did not support that simple rule. The picture was much more mixed.

So I'm not going to tell you that social media causes unhappiness, or that scrolling is proven to make you sad. The evidence doesn't support saying that. What I'll say is more modest. For some people, some of the time, the way they use these apps seems to affect how they feel. And the only way to know if that's you is to pay attention.

[pause]

So what does a better yardstick look like?

I'd suggest three.

The first is your own past self. Not where someone else is today, but where you were a year ago. What do you understand now that you didn't then? What conversations can you handle now that used to scare you?

The second is your own values. Why did you come into this work? If the answer is something like "to help families feel more prepared", then the right question at the end of a week isn't "did I do as well as them?" It's "did I live that out?"

The third is the clients you served well. The person who finally understood their situation. The family who felt calmer after you met. These things don't make it onto a stage, but they're the actual work.

Notice that none of these yardsticks need anyone else to lose for you to win. That's the trouble with the sideways look. It turns colleagues into a scoreboard. And in a profession where we often learn the most from each other, that's a real loss. When your yardstick is your own growth and your own clients, other people's good news can just be good news. You can be glad for them, and still feel steady about yourself.

And if you lead a team, this matters for them too. New advisers, especially, have very few yardsticks of their own yet. If all they see is other people's highlight reels, they'll measure themselves against those. Part of your job is to show them the behind-the-scenes. The slow weeks. The learning. The ordinary, steady work that sits underneath any good moment.

[pause]

Let me give you a picture.

Picture an adviser who scrolls through colleagues' posts every night in bed. Award stages, overseas conferences, "another record month". She falls asleep feeling behind, almost every night.

So she tries a small experiment. Just for two weeks. She mutes a few accounts. Not because she's angry with anyone, but because she's noticed how she feels after seeing them. She keeps social media to a set time during the day, not in bed. And each evening, she writes down one thing that went well with a client that day. Just one line.

After two weeks, something has shifted. She's still curious about what other people are doing. She still cheers for her colleagues. But she's measuring herself against her own month now, not someone else's best day. And she's sleeping a little better too, which, as we talked about two episodes ago, matters more than we often think.

[pause]

One more thing. If you post about your own work, this is worth thinking about from the other side. You don't need to stop sharing good news. But you might ask yourself who's watching, and what they'll feel. Sometimes a little honesty about the hard parts, too, can help someone else feel less alone.

[pause]

So here are three things to do this week.

First, each time you use social media this week, rate your mood afterwards, from one to five, in a note on your phone. Takes five seconds.

Second, at the end of the week, look at the pattern. Which times, which apps, which accounts leave you lower? Then change one thing. Mute, unfollow, set a time limit, or move your scrolling out of the bedroom.

Third, each evening, write one line about something that went well with a client. That's your new yardstick, in your own handwriting.

[pause]

Here's what I'd like you to carry this week. Don't try to stop comparing. Just change what you compare with. Your own past. Your own values. The people you helped.
