## Script

You had a good meeting last week. Warm conversation. Real interest. And now it's quiet.

So you're looking at your phone, thumb over the keyboard, wondering. Do I message again? Is it too soon? Too late? Am I being helpful, or am I being a nuisance?

And underneath that, there's a second question. Where's the line I actually can't cross?

[pause]

I'm Jason Ng, and this is Quiet Compounding.

This is an Agency Blueprint episode, so we're talking systems today. And the system is follow-up. Not the feeling of follow-up, which can be awkward. The structure of it.

Here's the one idea I want you to leave with. Follow-up is a service when it's agreed. It only becomes chasing when it isn't.

Let me unpack that.

When someone walks out of a meeting, they don't know when they'll hear from you, or how, or why. So every message you send after that is a small surprise. Some surprises are welcome. Some feel like pressure. You can't tell which in advance, and neither can they.

Now picture the same meeting, but in the last two minutes you ask a simple question. "When would you like to hear from me next, and what would be useful to talk about then?" And maybe a second one. "Is a text or an email easier for you?"

That's it. Now your next message isn't a surprise. It's something the person asked for. You're not chasing. You're keeping a promise.

That's the first piece. Agree the next contact before you leave. When, how, and about what.

The second piece is to have a default rhythm, and to write it down. Not in your head. On paper, or in your client notes.

Mine is simple. A short thank-you note soon after a meeting. A check-in at the time we agreed. And if that check-in gets no reply, one more gentle note later. Then a final message that closes the loop kindly and leaves the door open.

Something like, "I'll leave it here so I'm not crowding your inbox. If anything changes, I'm happy to pick up."

That last note matters more than you'd think. It tells the person they're free. And it tells you that you're free too. You stop carrying an open loop around in your head.

The third piece is the easiest to say and the most important to do. When someone says stop, you stop. Straight away. You record it, and you honour it. No "just one more". No clever workaround.

[pause]

Now let's talk about the floor. Because a rhythm that feels polite still has to sit on top of the rules.

I'll be careful here. What follows is my summary of what the regulators have published. It is not legal advice, and it is not a sign-off on how your firm does things. For that, check with your compliance team.

Start with the Personal Data Protection Commission, the PDPC. Its advisory guidelines on key concepts in the PDPA, revised in April twenty twenty-six, set out the Do Not Call rules. In plain words, if you're sending certain marketing calls or texts to Singapore phone numbers, you need to check the relevant Do Not Call register first. There are separate registers for calls, for texts, and for faxes. You need to say who sent or authorised the message. And you must not hide your number.

There's an exception to checking the register. It's where the person has given clear and unambiguous consent, in writing or another accessible form. Some messages within an ongoing relationship are also exempt. I'm not going to try to explain the scope of that exemption on a podcast. Ask your compliance team how your firm applies it.

And here's a detail I want you to hear clearly. The same PDPC guidelines say consent for direct marketing should be opt-in, like ticking a box that wasn't already ticked. Consent obtained by opt-out does not count as clear and unambiguous consent under the Do Not Call rules, for messaging a number on the register. So a pre-ticked box, or "you didn't say no", is not consent for that purpose.

Why does this matter? Partly because it's the right way to treat people. And partly because the stakes are real. The PDPC's guidelines on enforcement, revised in October twenty twenty-two, say that for breaches of the Do Not Call provisions, the financial penalty can be up to two hundred thousand dollars for an individual, and up to one million dollars in other cases. Those are maximums, not typical outcomes. But they tell you how seriously this is taken.

Then there's MAS. On the twelfth of October, twenty twenty-six, MAS published its response to feedback on safeguards for prospecting in public places and for telemarketing. In that response, MAS describes telemarketing as voice-only calls to retail customers without a prior appointment. For each of those calls, MAS says the customer's consent must be obtained afresh. Not once for all time. Each call.

Two things to note. First, this is still being finalised. MAS has said updated guidelines or notices are still to be issued, with a nine-month transition. So check with your compliance team on what applies to you, and when.

Second, text messages fall outside MAS's telemarketing definition. But please don't hear that as "texts are unregulated". They're not. The PDPA's Do Not Call duties still apply to marketing texts. Hold both of those together.

[pause]

Okay. So we've got the shape, and we've got the floor. Now, how do you make it actually happen on a busy week?

This is where a piece of research I like comes in. In two thousand and six, psychologists Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran published a meta-analysis of ninety-four independent tests on something called implementation intentions. That's just a fancy name for if-then plans. "If this situation happens, then I will do that."

They found these plans had a medium-to-large positive effect on people reaching their goals. Now, those studies covered many kinds of goals. None of them was about advisers. But the idea travels well.

Because follow-up rarely fails from a lack of good intentions. It fails at the handover moment. The meeting ends, you get in the car, another call comes in, and the agreed next step lives only in your memory.

So you make the handover automatic. "If a meeting ends, then I log the agreed next step before I leave the building." You don't need willpower for that. You need a trigger and a habit.

[pause]

Let me give you a picture of what this looks like.

Picture an adviser who used to send "just checking in" texts every few days to anyone who'd gone quiet. She dreaded every one of them. She felt like she was bothering people, and honestly, sometimes she was.

So she changed one thing first. She now ends every meeting with, "When would you like to hear from me next, and is text or email better?" She writes the answer in her client notes with a date, before she leaves.

When that date arrives, she sends the check-in. If there's no reply, she sends one more note a little later. And if that's quiet too, she sends her closing message. "I'll leave it here so I'm not crowding your inbox. If anything changes, I'm happy to pick up."

For anyone new, before any marketing message goes out, she follows her firm's process on Do Not Call checks and consent records. She doesn't guess.

What changed for her wasn't just her calendar. It was the feeling. Her follow-ups stopped being little acts of nerve. They became small, expected, respectful touches. And when someone went quiet, she let them, without taking it personally.

[pause]

So here are three things to do this week.

First, write your default follow-up rhythm on one page. The agreed next step, the check-in, and the closing note. Use your exact wording, so you're not inventing it under pressure each time. Read it back and ask, would I be comfortable receiving this?

Second, add one if-then plan. Just one. Something like, "If a meeting ends, then I log the next step before I leave." Stick it where you'll see it. Try it for the week and notice what slips through less.

Third, ask your compliance team how your firm handles Do Not Call checks and consent records. Ask how they treat texts as well as calls. And ask what they expect once the updated MAS guidance on telemarketing is issued. Write down what they tell you, so it's part of your system and not just a conversation.

[pause]

If this episode was about the structure of follow-up, the next one is about the person doing it. Episode fifty-four is on patience in a long cycle, and how to keep your energy up when people take months to decide.

Here's the line I'd like you to keep.

Agree the next step, keep the promise, and let silence be an answer.

Thanks for listening to Quiet Compounding. Content on this show is checked by my team, but it's general discussion, not advice for your situation. Take what's useful, check the rules with your compliance team, and I'll see you next time.
