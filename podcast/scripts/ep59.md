## Script

It's Monday morning. You sit down, open your laptop, and you have that slightly sick feeling. You know there were things you said you'd do last week. You told someone you'd call them back. You said you'd send something over. You promised a colleague you'd look at their case. But what exactly? And for whom? And by when?

So you start the week from scratch. Again. And somewhere out there, someone is waiting for a message from you that you've already forgotten.

That's what today is about. Not a new tool. Not a new app. Just a short, regular meeting with yourself that stops things slipping.

I'm Jason Ng, and this is Quiet Compounding.

[pause]

We're in our second theme, the working week and your energy. Two episodes ago, we gave every kind of work a home in your calendar. Last time, we talked about how habits form, through a cue and repetition, not willpower. Today we put both together, with one habit that holds a whole week in place.

The weekly review.

Here's the one idea. Once a week, same day, same time, for about thirty to forty-five minutes, you look back at what happened and you look ahead at what's next. You write it down. And every open promise leaves that meeting with a next step and a date.

That's it. It sounds almost too simple. But let me tell you why I think it works, and what the research says about the pieces of it.

As always, a quick caution first. The studies I'm going to mention looked at many kinds of goals, often things like health behaviours. They weren't studies of financial advisers. So I'll describe them as general research on goals, and you can decide how it fits your practice.

[pause]

Let's start with looking back.

In two thousand and sixteen, a group of researchers led by Benjamin Harkin published a large meta-analysis. They pulled together a hundred and thirty-eight studies, with nearly twenty thousand people in total. They looked at what happened when people were prompted to monitor their progress towards a goal.

What they found was that helping people track their progress helped them reach their goals. And two details stood out to me. The effect was larger when progress was physically recorded, written down rather than just kept in your head. And it was larger when the progress was reported to someone else, or made public.

So think about what that means for your review. Don't just think about last week. Write it down. And if you have someone you trust, an accountability partner, a mentor, a peer in your team, consider sharing a short summary with them.

When you look back, I'd ask three questions. What did I actually do? What did I promise, to clients, to prospects, to my team? And what's still open?

That middle question is the important one. Promises are where trust is built or quietly lost. Most of us aren't breaking promises on purpose. We're just losing track of them.

[pause]

Now, looking ahead.

There's a set of studies I find really helpful here, by E. J. Masicampo and Roy Baumeister, published in two thousand and eleven. They found that unfinished goals caused intrusive thoughts. They kept popping into people's minds, and they got in the way of other tasks. But when people were allowed to make a specific plan for those unfinished goals, those effects went away.

I love that finding. Because it means the review doesn't just help your list. It helps your head. You don't have to finish everything on Friday afternoon to feel lighter. You just have to decide, clearly, what you'll do next and when. The plan itself gives your mind permission to stop worrying about it.

So for every open loop, write a specific next step. Not "follow up with that prospect". Something like "Wednesday, two o'clock, send the summary I promised". Then put it in the calendar.

And for the most important ones, use the if-then plans we've talked about before. In two thousand and six, a review of ninety-four tests found these if-then plans had a medium-to-large positive effect on reaching goals. "If it's Wednesday at two, then I call the three people who asked me to follow up." The decision is already made. Future you just has to show up.

[pause]

Let me give you a picture.

Picture an adviser who blocks four o'clock every Friday. It's in her calendar as a recurring meeting, with herself.

She opens last week's calendar and her message threads, and she writes down every promise she made. "Send that comparison of options." "Call back after the school holidays." "Introduce him to my colleague." Things that would have been easy to forget.

Each one gets a next step and a date. She ticks what's done. And for anything that slipped, she writes a short note on why. Not to beat herself up. Just to notice patterns. Maybe Thursdays always get eaten by meetings. Maybe she keeps saying yes to things she doesn't have room for.

Then she sends a three-line summary to her accountability partner. What I finished. What's open. My three priorities for next week.

On Monday morning, she doesn't start from a blank page. She starts from a list. And that sick feeling from the start of the episode? It mostly isn't there.

[pause]

There's one more part of the review I want you to include, and it matters.

A compliance check. Are my notes filed? Are my follow-ups logged? Is there anything that needs my compliance team to look at?

But I want to be very clear about what this is and what it isn't. Your weekly review is a habit. It's a way of noticing what needs attention. It is not a compliance sign-off. It doesn't replace anything your firm requires. If you're not sure what's required, or when, check with your compliance team. The review just makes sure you remember to ask.

[pause]

A couple of practical tips before the actions.

Keep it short. Thirty to forty-five minutes is plenty. If it turns into a two-hour ordeal, you'll stop doing it. Remember what we said last week about habits. Small and repeated beats big and abandoned.

Keep the same slot. Same day, same time, same place if you can. That's your cue. Friday afternoon can work well because the week is fresh in your mind. You might prefer Monday morning. Pick one and protect it.

Keep the questions the same each week. You don't need to reinvent the review every Friday. The same three questions, in the same order, become a little routine of their own. After a few weeks, you'll find you answer them faster, because you've started noticing your promises as you make them.

Use what you already have. Your calendar shows what happened. Your message threads show what you said. Your notes show what's open. The review isn't about building a new system. It's about looking, once a week, at the systems you already have.

And if you miss a week, just do the next one. One missed review doesn't undo the habit.

[pause]

So here are three things to do this week.

First, book a recurring thirty-minute weekly review in your calendar, at the same slot every week. Give it a name you'll respect. "Weekly review" is fine.

Second, in your first one, answer three questions on paper. What did I finish? What did I promise that's still open? And what are my three next steps, and when will I do each one?

Third, add a short compliance check to the end. Notes filed, follow-ups logged, anything to raise with your compliance team. And if you have someone you trust, send them a three-line summary.

[pause]

If you lead others, there's a quiet bonus here. When your own promises are tracked, you model something for your team without saying a word. People notice the leader who always calls back when they said they would.

Here's what I'd like you to carry this week. Trust isn't only built in big moments. It's built in small promises, kept. The weekly review is simply how you keep track of them.

Next time, we turn to something that affects every promise you make, and every decision you take. Sleep, boundaries, and those messages that arrive at eleven at night.

Thanks for listening to Quiet Compounding. These are my personal views as a representative, and I'm not speaking on behalf of any company. It's general discussion, not advice for your situation. Take care, and I'll see you next time.
