## Script

It's the last week of the quarter. You've worked hard. And you're close. Not miles away. Just short. You can feel the gap. And this week, you've got a few client meetings lined up.

You'd never call yourself someone who cuts corners. You came into this work to help people. But you notice something. In one meeting, you talk a little faster than usual. You skip the line you normally say, the one where you ask, "Would you like some time to think about it?" Nobody asked you to skip it. You just did.

That's what today's episode is about. Not bad people doing bad things. Good people, under pressure, and the small ways pressure can bend our judgement without us quite noticing.

I'm Jason Ng, and this is Quiet Compounding.

[pause]

We're in our third theme, compliance, ethics and hard conversations. Last time we talked about compliance as a daily habit. Today we look at the person doing that habit, and what happens when the pressure goes up.

Here's the one idea I want you to take away. Pressure changes the odds. So decide your rules while you're calm, before the pressure arrives. And say the pressure out loud to someone you trust.

[pause]

Let's start with what the research says. And as always, I'll be careful. These studies weren't done with financial advisers. So I'll tell you what they found, in the settings they were done in, and you can think about what it means for you.

In two thousand and four, three researchers, Maurice Schweitzer, Lisa Ordóñez and Bambi Douma, ran a lab experiment on goals and honesty. They found that people who had goals they hadn't met were more likely to behave unethically than people who were simply told to do their best. That held whether or not there was money attached. And here's the part that stays with me. The effect was particularly strong when people were just short of their goals.

Just short. Not far behind. Close. Exactly where you might be in the last week of a quarter.

[pause]

A few years later, in two thousand and nine, some of the same researchers, with colleagues, wrote an article with a memorable title. "Goals Gone Wild." It's not a new experiment. It's a perspective piece, looking across research on goal setting. And its argument was that the benefits of goals have been overstated, and that goals can have side effects.

They listed several. Narrow focus, where you see the target and miss everything else. Changes in how much risk people are willing to take. A rise in unethical behaviour. Less learning. Damage to culture. And less of the inner motivation that brought people to the work in the first place.

Now, they didn't say goals are bad. They said goals need careful dosing. I think that's a fair way to put it. Goals can help us. But we should use them knowing they have side effects, the way you'd read the label on anything strong.

[pause]

There's one more piece I want to mention, briefly, because we touched on it a few episodes ago.

A study from two thousand and eleven, by Christopher Barnes and colleagues, linked less sleep to lower self-control, and to more unethical behaviour, in lab studies, and in a study of people at work. I'll add a note of caution here. The theory behind that research has been debated by other scientists since then. So I'd describe it as one line of research, not settled fact. And it certainly doesn't mean tired advisers are dishonest.

But put it next to the end of a quarter. Long days. Late meetings. Less sleep. And a target just out of reach. That's not a moral failing. It's a set of conditions. And conditions can be planned for.

[pause]

Now, what does MAS say about this?

In its Guidelines on Fair Dealing, from May twenty twenty-four, MAS describes a poor practice. In plain words, it's when a representative skews recommendations towards options that would earn them a higher fee or commission. MAS is clear that this is not fair dealing.

The same guidelines also suggest that firms consider a balanced scorecard approach, one that takes into account conduct-related factors as well as financial targets, so that the way people are paid doesn't create conflicts that hurt customers.

So the direction from the regulator is plain. Fair dealing comes before the target. If you ever feel those two pulling in different directions, that's your signal to slow down, and to check with your compliance team or your leader.

[pause]

So how do you protect yourself, in practice?

I'd suggest three ideas.

The first is to know your danger zones. For many of us, it's the end of a quarter, or the end of a campaign period, or late at night when we're tired. Everyone's danger zones are a little different. Take a moment and think. When do I feel the pull most?

The second is to set your rules in advance, while you're calm. Not in the meeting, when the pressure is on. In a quiet week, when you can think clearly. Rules like "I always offer every client time to decide", or "I don't hold client meetings after a certain hour". When the pressure comes, you don't have to decide what kind of adviser to be. You already decided.

And the third, which I think is the most powerful, is to say it out loud. Tell a leader, a peer, or someone you trust, "I'm close to target, and I notice the pull." It sounds almost too simple. But naming the pressure takes away much of its power. It moves it from a private feeling into something you can look at together.

[pause]

Let me give you a picture.

Picture an adviser who notices a pattern in himself. In the last fortnight of every quarter, something shifts. He talks faster in meetings. He skips the "do you want to think about it?" line. He doesn't feel like a different person. But looking back, he can see it.

So in a calm week, in the middle of the quarter, he sits down and writes himself three rules.

One. In the last fortnight, he still offers every client time to decide. Every single one.

Two. He doesn't book client meetings after nine at night, however tempting the gap in his numbers.

Three. Every Monday, he tells his team leader where he stands. Plainly. No spin.

The next quarter comes. The pressure is still there. It doesn't go away. He still wants to reach his goal. But now the pressure doesn't run the meeting. His rules do. And he walks out of each meeting knowing that whatever the client decided, they decided it with time and clear information.

[pause]

If you lead a team, there's something for you here too. The way you talk about targets matters. If the last week of every quarter is full of urgency and countdowns, your team will feel it in their meetings. You can still care about results, and still make it clear, out loud, that how we get there matters more. Ask your team about their pressure rules. Share your own.

[pause]

So here are three things to do this week.

First, write down your own danger zones. The times and situations when you feel the pull most.

Second, write two or three pressure rules. Lines you'll keep, no matter where you are against target. Keep them short, and keep them somewhere you'll see them.

Third, share those rules with one person you trust. A leader, a peer, or a partner. And agree that, near the end of a quarter, you'll tell them honestly how it's going.

[pause]

Here's what I'd like you to carry this week. The target is a number. The client is a person. When those two pull apart, choose the person, every time.

Next time, we'll look at social media. How to share your work online without regret, and what MAS has said about digital advertising.

Thanks for listening to Quiet Compounding. These are my personal views as a representative, and I'm not speaking on behalf of any company. It's general discussion, not compliance or legal advice, and not advice for your situation. This episode was checked by my team. Take care, and I'll see you next time.
