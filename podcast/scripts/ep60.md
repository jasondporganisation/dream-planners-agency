## Script

It's eleven o'clock at night. You're finally in bed. The lights are off. And then your phone lights up on the pillow next to you. A client. "Hi, sorry to bother you, quick question."

And you feel it. That pull. If I don't reply now, will they think I don't care? Will they go to someone else? So you reply. And then they reply. And it's midnight, and you're wide awake, and your first meeting tomorrow is at nine.

If that sounds familiar, this episode is for you.

Before we begin, one important note. I'm not a doctor, and this isn't medical advice. If problems with your sleep, or with stress, go on for weeks, please see a doctor. And if you need someone to talk to, you can call National Mindline on one seven seven one, or the Samaritans of Singapore on one seven six seven. Both are open twenty-four hours.

I'm Jason Ng, and this is Quiet Compounding.

[pause]

We're in our second theme, the working week and your energy. We've talked about burnout, time-blocking, habits, and the weekly review. Today we look at the edges of the working day. The hours when work is supposed to stop, but often doesn't.

Here's the one idea I want you to leave with. Your sleep is not separate from your work. It's part of how you do your work well. And protecting it doesn't mean letting clients down. It means setting clear expectations, and keeping them.

[pause]

Let's start with why sleep matters for us in particular.

There's a study from two thousand and eleven, by Christopher Barnes and colleagues, that I think every adviser should know about. In lab studies, they found that getting more sleep was linked to having more self-control, and to less unethical behaviour. Then they looked at real workplaces. In a field study, low sleep quantity and poor sleep quality were linked to unethical behaviour, as rated by people's supervisors. And another study, which tracked people over time, found similar effects within the same individuals.

Now I want to be careful. That study wasn't done with financial advisers in Singapore. And it doesn't mean that a tired adviser will do something wrong. That would be stretching it too far. What it does tell us is that sleep and self-control seem to be connected. And in a profession built on trust and careful judgement, that's worth taking seriously.

Think about what our work asks of us. Listening carefully. Explaining things clearly. Resisting the shortcut. Asking the extra question. Saying "let me check" instead of guessing. All of that needs a clear head.

[pause]

So why is it so hard to put the phone down?

Researchers have a name for that pull. They call it workplace telepressure. It means being preoccupied with work messages, and feeling an urge to respond to them quickly. In a twenty fifteen paper, Larissa Barber and Alecia Santuzzi studied it. They found that telepressure predicted burnout, absence from work, and poorer sleep quality, over and above other features of people's jobs.

I find it helpful just to have a word for it. Because once you can name it, you can notice it. That tight feeling when the phone buzzes. The voice that says "reply now, or else". That's telepressure. It's not proof that something urgent is happening. It's just a feeling.

[pause]

Now, how much sleep are we talking about?

Singapore's Health Promotion Board, on HealthHub, says that if you're over eighteen, you should aim for about seven hours of sleep. It also suggests avoiding electronics, like your phone, for at least an hour before bed. The MindSG pages on HealthHub say something similar. Adults should aim for at least seven hours a day. And reading emails, texting, or scrolling social media before bed can make it harder to fall asleep and stay asleep.

So when you're replying to clients in bed at midnight, it's not just the minutes you lose. It's what the phone does to the sleep that follows.

[pause]

There's one more piece of context that's specific to many of us.

IRAS, Singapore's tax authority, lists commission agents among self-employed people, who declare their commission as self-employment income. If that describes you, it means something for your evenings. Rules or policies that apply to employees about after-hours contact may not apply to you in the same way. In other words, nobody is going to set your boundaries for you. They're mostly yours to set.

Everyone's arrangement is different, so do check your own contract, and your firm's guidance. But for many of us, the honest truth is this. If we don't decide when the working day ends, it doesn't end.

[pause]

Let me give you a picture of what this can look like.

Picture an adviser who keeps her phone on the pillow, so she never misses a client. She replies after midnight more often than she'd like to admit. And she wakes up foggy, rushing into morning meetings feeling half there.

One week, she decides to change it. She adds a line to the welcome message she sends new clients. "I reply to messages between nine in the morning and eight at night. For anything urgent, here's who to contact." She works out with her team, and checks with her compliance team, what that urgent route should be. Then she sets an auto-reply for late evenings that says the same thing, kindly. And she starts charging her phone in the living room, not next to her bed.

A few clients test it. A message at ten. Another at eleven. They get the auto-reply. And most of them simply wait until morning. Because most of those late messages were never really urgent. They were just the moment the client happened to think of it.

What changed for her? She sleeps better. She walks into her nine o'clock meeting with a clear head. And her clients still feel looked after, maybe more so, because they know exactly when they'll hear from her, and what to do if it can't wait.

[pause]

I want to be clear about one thing. This is not "never answer clients after hours". Some situations really can't wait. The point is to set clear hours, and a clear route for anything urgent, so you're not on call every hour of every day by default. You decide what's right for your practice. And then you keep to it, so your clients can trust it.

A few simple tools can help. An auto-reply after hours. Scheduled sending, so a message you write at night arrives in the morning instead, and doesn't teach clients to expect midnight replies. "Do not disturb" settings on your phone. And, honestly, the most powerful one: the phone outside the bedroom.

And if you lead a team, think about what your own habits teach them. If the leader sends messages at midnight, new advisers quickly learn that midnight is part of the job. If the leader keeps clear hours and still serves clients well, they learn something healthier. Your boundary gives them permission to have one too.

One last thought on this. Boundaries can feel selfish at first, especially in a job about caring for people. But a rested adviser listens better, explains better, and catches more of what matters. Looking after your sleep is one of the ways you look after your clients.

[pause]

So here are three things to do this week.

First, choose your last reply time. The time after which, on a normal day, you stop answering work messages. Write it down.

Second, draft a short after-hours auto-reply. Say when you'll respond, and where to go for urgent help. Keep it warm and clear. Then check the wording with your compliance team before you switch it on.

Third, for one week, charge your phone outside your bedroom. Use an old-fashioned alarm clock if you need one. And notice how you feel in the mornings.

[pause]

Here's what I'd like you to carry this week. Being reachable all the time is not the same as being reliable. Reliable is clear hours, a clear route for urgent things, and a rested mind when you show up.

Next time, we turn to something many of us find uncomfortable to talk about. Money habits. Specifically, how to handle your own money when your income doesn't arrive in the same amount every month.

Thanks for listening to Quiet Compounding. These are my personal views as a representative, and I'm not speaking on behalf of any company. It's general discussion, not advice for your situation. Sleep well, and I'll see you next time.
