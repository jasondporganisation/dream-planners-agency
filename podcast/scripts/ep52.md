## Script

It's Friday night. Two appointments cancelled this week. A client you were sure about has gone quiet. And there's a voice in your head that sounds very sure of itself.

"I'm useless at this. It's never going to work."

So here's the question. Does that voice actually matter? Or is it just noise you push through?

[pause]

I'm Jason Ng, and this is Quiet Compounding. A show for financial advisers in Singapore about building a practice, and the person behind it.

This is a Resilient Advisor episode. That's the track about you, the person doing the work. Last episode, on The Agency Blueprint track, we talked about tracking a few leading numbers every week. And I said a bad week of numbers is information, not a verdict. Today is about that second part. What you tell yourself when the week goes badly.

Psychologists have a name for this. Explanatory style. It's the habitual way you explain bad events to yourself. Not the event itself. The story you attach to it.

And they look at it through three simple questions.

One. Is it about me? Psychologists call that internal versus external. Was this all down to me, or did circumstances play a part?

Two. Will it last? That's stable versus temporary. Is this how it will be forever, or is it this week?

Three. Does it affect everything? That's global versus specific. Does this spill into every part of my work and life, or is it one area?

A pessimistic style answers all three the same way. "It's me. It's permanent. It ruins everything." You can hear it in the Friday night voice. "I'm useless" is the first one. "It's never going to work" is the second. And the feeling that your whole career is in question, that's the third.

A more optimistic style sounds different. "Part of this was circumstance. It's this week. It's this one area."

[pause]

Now, I want to be very clear about what this is not.

This is not positive thinking. It's not pretending a bad week was a good one. It's not ignoring your mistakes. Sometimes part of a bad week really is down to you. Maybe you didn't confirm a meeting. Maybe your follow-up was slow. That's worth knowing.

The aim is accuracy. An explanation that's true, and that leaves you able to act. "I'm useless" isn't accurate, and it gives you nothing to do. "My follow-up was slow this week" is accurate, and it gives you something to fix on Monday.

Think about how much of our work involves hearing no, or not yet, or nothing at all. Meetings move. People need time. Some conversations simply don't go anywhere, and that's fine. If every one of those moments gets filed under "proof that I'm not good enough", the weight builds up quietly. Week after week. And it becomes harder to pick up the phone on Monday.

But if each one gets an honest, specific explanation, it stays the size it actually is. A cancelled meeting stays a cancelled meeting. It doesn't grow into a judgement about your whole career.

So, does the voice matter? There's some research worth knowing about here, and I'll describe it carefully.

In nineteen eighty-six, the psychologists Seligman and Schulman published a study of sales agents. Their starting point was a theory: that explaining bad events as internal, permanent and all-encompassing makes people more likely to give up when bad things keep happening. And they chose to test it in a job where setbacks are frequent. They measured explanatory style with a questionnaire.

In the first part, with ninety-four experienced agents, those who scored in the more optimistic half sold noticeably more over their first two years than those in the less optimistic half. In the second part, they followed one hundred and three newly hired agents for a year. Those in the more optimistic half when they were hired stayed in the job at twice the rate of the other half, and sold more.

Here's the honest caveat. That's one study, in one sales setting, from four decades ago. It shows an association. It doesn't prove that optimism causes results, and it isn't a promise about anyone's results today. What I take from it is narrower. How you explain setbacks to yourself seems to be connected to whether you keep going.

And the more useful question is whether explanatory style can change. Because if it's fixed, this is just a personality test.

There's encouraging research on that too. A controlled trial published in two thousand and nine, by Proudfoot and colleagues, worked with one hundred and sixty-six financial services sales agents. They were randomly assigned either to a seven-week cognitive-behavioural training programme or to a waiting list. Cognitive-behavioural, in plain terms, means learning to notice your thoughts, test them against the evidence, and respond differently.

The agents who did the training showed an improved explanatory style. They also showed better job satisfaction, self-esteem and psychological well-being, and better general productivity. And fewer of them left over the four and a half months that followed.

Again, one trial, in one setting. But it points in a hopeful direction. The way you talk to yourself is not fixed. It's closer to a skill. And skills can be practised.

[pause]

So what does practice look like? Let me show you.

Picture an adviser who has had a rough week. Two appointments cancelled. A client he'd been working with for a while has gone quiet. On Friday night he's sitting at home, and his first thought is, "I'm not cut out for this. Nothing ever works for me."

Instead of just sitting with that, he tries the three questions.

Is it all me? Well, one of the cancellations was because the client's child was sick. That's not about him.

Is it permanent? Last month was fine. Actually, last month was steady.

Does it affect everything? His existing clients are happy. He had a good review meeting on Wednesday.

Then he asks one more question. Is any of this genuinely mine to fix? And he's honest. Yes. He was slow to follow up with the client who's gone quiet. He also didn't confirm either of the cancelled meetings the day before.

So he rewrites the story. "This week was thin. Partly bad luck, and partly my follow-up was slow. Next week, I'll confirm every meeting the day before, and I'll call the quiet client on Monday."

Notice what changed. It's still a bad week. He doesn't pretend otherwise. But it's no longer a verdict on his life. It's a thin week with one clear thing to do.

[pause]

One more thing before the actions, and it matters.

Everything I've described is for the ordinary bad week. The kind that comes with this work. But if the inner voice turns to hopelessness, and stays there for more than a short while, please don't carry it alone. Speak to someone. A friend, a mentor, a doctor, a counsellor. That's what support is for.

In Singapore, Samaritans of Singapore has a twenty-four hour hotline on one seven six seven. They also have a twenty-four hour CareText messaging service, at nine one five one, one seven six seven. And if you or anyone else is in immediate danger, call nine nine five or nine nine nine.

[pause]

So here are three things to do this week.

First, after your next disappointing day, write three lines. Just three. Line one: what happened, in plain facts. Line two: your first explanation, exactly as the voice said it. Don't tidy it up.

Second, write a more accurate version, using the three questions. Me, or circumstance? This week, or forever? This one area, or everything? Look for evidence on both sides. Be fair to yourself, and be honest about the part that's yours.

Third, end with one action that's in your control. Something small and specific you can do in the next few days. Confirm a meeting. Make a call. Block time for follow-ups. Then do it, and notice how it feels to act instead of just replaying the week. If you're tracking leading numbers from last episode, this is often where the two habits meet. The numbers show you where the week went thin, and this practice keeps you from turning that into a story about who you are.

That's it. A pen, three lines, and one action.

[pause]

If you only remember one line from today, let it be this. A bad week is something that happened to you; it's not who you are.

Thanks for listening to Quiet Compounding. These are my personal views, shared for advisers, and I'm not speaking on behalf of any company. The studies I mentioned are in the show notes. And if this episode helped, think of one colleague who's had a rough week, and check in on them.

Until next time, keep it quiet, and keep it compounding.
