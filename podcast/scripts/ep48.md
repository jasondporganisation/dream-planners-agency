## Script

There's a moment some of us know too well. You walk into a family dinner, or a friend's birthday, and you catch yourself wondering. Are they bracing themselves? Are they waiting for me to bring up work? Am I becoming that person? The one everyone quietly avoids at gatherings.

If you've ever felt that, I want you to know you're not strange for feeling it. And there's a way through.

[pause]

I'm Jason Ng, and this is Quiet Compounding. A show for financial advisory professionals in Singapore, about building the practice and the person behind it. This is The Resilient Advisor track, where we look after the person doing the work. These are my personal views as a representative, shared with fellow advisers, and I'm not speaking on behalf of any company. General information, not legal or compliance advice.

Today's idea is this. "That person" is defined by behaviour, not by job title. And behaviour is something you can choose.

But before we get to the fixing, let's start with the fear itself.

[pause]

The fear is reasonable. I'd go further. It usually comes from care. You don't want to lose the warmth of your relationships. You don't want your cousin to feel cornered. You don't want your old friends to start screening your calls. That's not weakness. That's someone who values people.

So the first step is simply to name it. "I'm worried my friends will see me as a salesperson." Say it plainly, to yourself. A fear you can name is a fear you can work with.

And notice what the fear is protecting. Usually it's protecting something good. Trust. Belonging. The feeling of being welcome at the table. Those are worth protecting. The aim isn't to get rid of the fear by pretending it's silly. The aim is to give it less to be afraid of. When the people around you can see that you respect their choices, the fear has less to hold on to.

Now, here's something that may help. Researchers have studied how accurately we judge the way we come across to others. And the findings are quite gentle.

In two thousand, a set of five studies published in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology looked at what's called the spotlight effect. People overestimated how much others noticed their actions and appearance. In one study, participants wore a potentially embarrassing T-shirt, and they overestimated how many observers would remember it. The authors suggested that we anchor on our own experience, and we don't adjust enough for the fact that other people are busy thinking about themselves.

Then, in twenty eighteen, a paper in Psychological Science looked at something called the liking gap. After conversations, people consistently underestimated how much their conversation partners liked them. This was found with strangers in a lab, with first-year university students who shared dorms, and with members of the public at a workshop. Among the dorm mates, the gap lasted several months.

And a twenty fourteen paper in the Journal of Experimental Psychology described field experiments with commuters on trains and buses. People who were asked to talk to a stranger reported a more positive commute than those who stayed silent. And it was no less productive. Meanwhile, other participants had predicted the opposite. The authors linked this partly to people underestimating how much others are interested in connecting.

Now, I want to be careful here. These studies were done with students, commuters and members of the public. Not with advisers. And none of them was a Singapore study. So I'm not telling you your friends definitely like you more than you think. What I am saying is this. Studies found that people tend to judge themselves more harshly than others do. Your worst guess about how you come across may be too harsh.

[pause]

So if the fear is often bigger than reality, what actually makes someone "that person"? In my view, it comes down to three behaviours.

One. Pushing. Pressing when the other person hasn't shown interest.

Two. Not hearing "no". Treating a polite decline as an objection to get around.

Three. Turning every chat into a pitch. The wedding, the reunion, the hospital visit. Everything becomes an opening.

Here's the good news. You can avoid all three. Your job title doesn't make you that person. Those behaviours do.

So what do you do instead? Be upfront, and give an easy exit.

Say what you do and why you're asking. Then make "no" genuinely easy. That protects the relationship, because nobody feels tricked, and nobody feels trapped.

It's worth noting that MAS is moving in this same direction for public places. In a response paper published in October twenty twenty-six, MAS said it will require representatives at public places to tell people upfront that they intend to market, and to get their consent before starting any engagement. MAS cited complaints that representatives were "not forthcoming about their identities and intentions". Those requirements are still to be issued, so check with your compliance team on the details and timing. But the spirit is clear. Say who you are. Say what you want. Let people choose.

The second thing that helps is separating the friendship from the business. Out loud. Here's a line you could adapt.

"I'd love to tell you about what I do one day, but only if it's useful. Either way, nothing changes between us."

And then mean it. If they say no, nothing changes. Same jokes. Same invitations. Same you.

That last part is where trust is really built. Not in what you say, but in what happens afterwards. When someone says no, and the next time you meet you're exactly as warm as before, they learn something about you. They learn that it's safe to be honest with you. And over time, that safety is worth far more than any single conversation.

The third thing is my own view, not a research finding. You don't have to start with your closest people. It can be easier to build your skill and your confidence with warm-but-not-close contacts first. People you know, people who know you, but where the stakes don't feel quite so high. Your closest relationships can wait until you feel steadier. Or they can simply stay friendships. That's allowed too.

[pause]

Let me paint a picture.

Picture an adviser who skips his cousin's birthday dinner, because he's sure everyone expects a pitch. He replays old conversations in his head. He imagines eye-rolls that may never have happened.

A mentor suggests a small test. At the next gathering, talk about anything but work, unless someone asks. And if someone does ask, give one honest sentence about what you do, and add, "No pressure, I'm off duty tonight."

So he goes. He talks about football, and food, and his niece's school. Late in the evening, someone asks what he's been up to. He gives his one sentence, and his easy exit.

Two relatives ask follow-up questions. Nobody avoids him.

The fear doesn't vanish. I won't pretend it does. But it shrinks to a size he can work with. And he gets his family dinners back.

[pause]

So, three things for this week.

First. Write your one-sentence answer to "So, what do you do?" Make it honest. Make it short. And build in an easy exit, so the other person can smile and move on without any awkwardness.

Second. Say it out loud three times. In the car, in the shower, wherever. It sounds small, but saying it aloud takes some of the charge out of it. You'll hear what sounds natural and what sounds forced, and you can adjust.

Third. Use it once this week. Only when someone asks. Don't steer the conversation towards it. Just let it be there when the moment comes, and notice how the other person actually responds, rather than how you feared they would.

[pause]

One more thing, before we close. If the anxiety around this is strong, or it's been with you for a long time, it's worth talking to someone. A mentor, a friend, or a professional. That's a strength, not a failure. And if you're ever struggling and need someone right away, Samaritans of Singapore has a twenty-four-hour hotline at one seven six seven. They also run a twenty-four-hour CareText messaging service, at nine one five one, one seven six seven. And if you or someone else is in immediate danger, call nine nine five or nine nine nine.

[pause]

If you caught last episode, on The Agency Blueprint track, we talked about building a prospecting system that doesn't lean on friends and family. This episode is the other half. A system gives you somewhere to go. Kindness towards yourself makes it possible to keep going.

Here's the line I'd like you to leave with.

You don't stop being their friend when you start being an adviser, unless you act like you have.

Thanks for listening to Quiet Compounding. I'm Jason Ng. Be gentle with yourself this week, and I'll see you next time.
