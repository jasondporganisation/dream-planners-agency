## Script

Some people decide in a week. Some take months.

And if you've been in this work for any length of time, you know the feeling of the months. The conversation that went well in spring and is still "thinking about it" now. The urge to send one more nudge. And then the small sting of resenting yourself for feeling that urge.

So how do you stay patient, and keep your energy up, without pushing anyone?

[pause]

I'm Jason Ng, and this is Quiet Compounding.

This is a Resilient Advisor episode, so today is less about systems and more about the person running them. You.

Here's the idea I want to build on. A long decision isn't a problem to fix. It's the client's right. And patience gets a lot easier when your attention is on what you control, rather than on the date someone else decides.

Let's start with the first part.

Important choices take time. They touch money, family, health, the future. People need to talk to a spouse, sleep on it, sit with a worry, come back to it. A slow yes is still a yes. A slow no is still a perfectly good answer. Neither one is a failure on your part.

When we forget that, we start treating someone's thinking time as an obstacle. And the moment we do, pressure creeps in. We get a little more frequent. A little more urgent. People can feel it, even when we think we're hiding it.

So the first shift is simple. Respect the pace. Let it belong to them.

[pause]

The second part is where your energy goes.

You can't control when a person decides. You can control your activity, your follow-up rhythm, and your learning. If those are where your attention lives, you'll have a decent week whether or not anyone said yes.

Think about what happens when it's the other way round. Your mood rises and falls with other people's replies. A quiet inbox feels like a verdict on you. A slow week feels like proof you're doing something wrong. And that's exactly when the urge to push gets loudest.

So try a small swap. Instead of asking, "Did anyone decide this week?", ask, "Did I do the things that were mine to do?" Did I have the conversations I planned? Did I keep the check-ins I promised? Did I learn one thing that makes me a little better at this work? Those questions have answers you can actually own.

In the companion episode, episode fifty-three, I talked about agreeing the next contact at the end of every conversation. That's what makes patience practical. If you've agreed to check in on a certain date, you don't need to decide every morning whether to nudge. The answer is already written down. Wait until the date.

Now, at this point someone usually says, "Jason, isn't this just about grit? Push through, keep going, don't quit?"

I want to be careful here, because grit is a popular idea, and the research is more mixed than the slogans.

In two thousand and seven, Angela Duckworth and colleagues published a paper defining grit as perseverance and passion for long-term goals. Across six samples, things like school attainment, cadet retention at a military academy, and spelling bee rankings, grit accounted for an average of four percent of the variance in those outcomes. It overlapped heavily with conscientiousness, though it added a little beyond that and beyond IQ.

Then, in twenty seventeen, a large meta-analysis by Marcus Credé and colleagues pulled together eighty-eight samples, more than sixty-six thousand people. They found grit was only moderately related to performance and retention, and very strongly related to conscientiousness. The part that looked more useful was what they called perseverance of effort, the keep-working part, more than consistency of interest. And they suggested that trying to raise grit might have only weak effects on performance.

None of these studies was about advisers or sales cycles. But here's what I take from them. Grit isn't a magic trait. Steady, reasonable effort on things you control seems to matter more than gritting your teeth at one outcome.

[pause]

Which brings me to something people don't often say out loud. Persistence is not the same as refusing to let go.

In two thousand and six, Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran published a meta-analysis of ninety-four tests on if-then plans. "If this happens, then I'll do that." They found a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals. And one detail I love is this. Those plans helped people start on their goals, shield them from distraction, and also disengage from failing courses of action, so they had capacity left for future goals.

That's patience done well. Not holding on forever. Knowing, kindly and calmly, when to close a file.

Closing a file isn't giving up on a person. It's giving them space, and giving yourself space too. The door stays open. You just stop standing in it.

[pause]

The third part is energy. And this is the one I've had to relearn more than once.

Energy comes from recovery, not just effort. In two thousand and seven, Sabine Sonnentag and Charlotte Fritz developed a questionnaire on recovery from work, tested with nine hundred and thirty people in total. They found four distinct recovery experiences in leisure time. Psychological detachment, which means actually switching off from work in your head. Relaxation. Mastery, which means learning something new or taking on a challenge outside work. And control, meaning you decide how to spend your own time.

That study describes kinds of recovery. It doesn't prove any one activity will restore your energy. But it's a useful map. If you're always half-checking messages on a Sunday night, you're not getting detachment. If every hour is someone else's request, you're not getting control.

And notice that rest isn't a reward you earn at the end of a good month. It's part of what lets you show up calm in the next conversation. I know that when I am tired, I read silence as rejection, and I am tempted to fill it with one message too many.

Two more things from my own view, not from a study.

Spread your pipeline, so no single person's decision carries your whole month. When one conversation holds all your hopes, patience is almost impossible. When you have several gentle conversations going, each one can move at its own pace.

And mark small progress. Notice the conversations you held this week. Notice what you learned. A decision is one moment. The work that leads to it is many.

[pause]

Let me give you a picture.

Picture an adviser with a prospective client who has been "thinking about it" for four months. He feels the urge to send another nudge. And he resents feeling it.

So he steps back. He remembers he agreed a check-in date with this person, and it hasn't arrived yet. He leaves it until then.

Meanwhile, he puts his effort into things he controls. Two new conversations this week. One skill he's been meaning to learn. He keeps Sunday evenings free of work messages, and he goes back to a hobby he'd dropped a while ago.

When the check-in date comes, he asks one simple question. "Is this still something you want to look at, or shall I close the file for now?"

And here's the important part. Either answer is fine. If the person wants to continue, great, they carry on at their pace. If they'd rather close it, he thanks them, closes it, and means it. He isn't keeping score. He's keeping his energy.

[pause]

So, three things for this week.

First, list every open conversation that's older than sixty days. Next to each one, write the agreed next contact date. Just look at the list. You might find it's shorter, or calmer, than the worry made it feel.

Second, for any conversation without an agreed date, plan one respectful question that lets the person continue or close. Something like, "Is this still something you'd like to look at, or shall I close the file for now?" No deadline, no urgency. Just a clear, kind choice.

Third, block one evening this week that's fully off work. No messages, no quick checks. Use it for detachment, for rest, for learning something unrelated, or simply for doing what you choose. Notice how you feel the next morning.

[pause]

Here's the line I'd like you to carry with you.

Respect their pace, tend your own effort, and let rest be part of the work.

Thanks for listening to Quiet Compounding. Content on this show is checked by my team, and it's general discussion, not advice for your situation. If you missed it, episode fifty-three covers follow-up rhythm and the rules around it. Look after yourself this week, and I'll see you next time.
