## Script

Your contact list is getting shorter. You've spoken to your old classmates. You've spoken to your cousins. You've had coffee with the people from your last job. And now there's a quiet question sitting in the back of your mind. Where do my next conversations come from, without leaning on the people I love?

If that's where you are, this episode is for you.

[pause]

I'm Jason Ng, and this is Quiet Compounding. A show for financial advisory professionals in Singapore, about building the practice and the person behind it. This is The Agency Blueprint track, where we talk about systems, numbers and structure. These are my personal views as a representative, for fellow advisers, and I'm not speaking on behalf of any company. It's general information, not legal or compliance advice.

Today's idea is simple. A warm list is a starting point. It is not a system.

Let me explain what I mean.

When you start out, your warm list does a lot of work. People know you. They trust you a little. They'll take your call. That's a real gift, and there's nothing wrong with starting there.

But if it's your only source, two things happen. First, it runs out. Second, every approach carries personal weight. Each call to a friend isn't just a business call. It's a small withdrawal from a relationship you care about. And when that's the only well you can draw from, you start to dread going back to it.

A system is different. A system is a few channels you can repeat every week, steadily, without that knot in your stomach.

What could those channels be? Here are four to think about.

One. People who already know your work. Clients who are glad they met you, and who might mention you to someone when it fits. Not because you pushed. Because you were useful.

Two. Community and interest groups you genuinely belong to. A sports club. A volunteer group. A faith community. A hobby circle. The key word is genuinely. You're there because you care about the thing, not because it's a hunting ground.

Three. Talks or workshops. A small group session on a money-and-life topic that people choose to attend.

Four. Content that helps people. A short note, an article, a simple explainer that someone is glad to read.

You don't need all four. Pick two or three. Then do them steadily. Steady beats busy.

[pause]

Now, before you try any channel, you need to know the rules for it. This part matters, so I'll go slowly.

In October twenty twenty-six, MAS published its response to feedback on safeguards for prospecting at public places and telemarketing. Here's what MAS said in that paper.

MAS will keep the ban on street canvassing and door-to-door prospecting. That ban first came in back in twenty twenty. MAS pointed to continued consumer complaints about harassment and unprofessional conduct. For door-to-door, it also pointed to people's residential space being intruded on. And street canvassing means approaching passers-by in pedestrian areas. That includes near MRT stations, bus interchanges and malls. So that's off the table. Full stop.

For other prospecting at public places, MAS has said it will require representatives to tell people upfront that they intend to market to them. And to get their consent before starting any engagement. MAS also said that pre-registration for an event may count as consent, where it was clear at sign-up that marketing would take place.

MAS also said the updated guidelines will not apply to pre-arranged appointments in public places, where the customer agreed in advance and knows what the meeting is for.

Now here's the important part. As of that paper, the updated guidelines and the new notices had not yet been issued. MAS said there will be a nine-month transition period from when they are issued. So this is still pending. Keep an eye on the MAS website, and check with your compliance team on what applies to you, and when.

What about phone calls? In the same paper, MAS said that for telemarketing, meaning voice-only calls without a prior appointment, consent must be obtained afresh for each call. MAS also said text messages fall outside its definition of telemarketing.

But that doesn't mean texts are a free-for-all. The PDPA's Do Not Call rules still apply. The PDPC's advisory guidelines, revised in April twenty twenty-six, say that organisations sending certain marketing messages to Singapore numbers must check the relevant Do Not Call register. That covers calls, texts and faxes. They must identify who sent or authorised the message. And they must not hide the calling number. You don't need to check the register where the person has given clear and unambiguous consent, in writing or another accessible form.

So, in plain words. Say who you are. Say why you're reaching out. Don't hide your number. Get consent. And on top of all of this, your own agency has its own rules. Those sit on top of the MAS and PDPC rules. Before you try a new channel, check with your compliance team.

[pause]

Here's the thing I find freeing about all this. The rules point in the same direction as good practice. Be upfront. Be welcome. Don't ambush anyone.

Which brings me to the heart of a good system. Lead with usefulness.

The aim of a first touch is not a sale. The aim is a conversation the other person is glad to have. If someone walks away from a talk you gave thinking, "That was helpful, I learned something," you've done your job for that day. Whether they become a client is a separate question, for another day, and it's theirs to answer.

And when you measure your week, measure the system, not just the outcome. Count conversations started. Not only meetings closed. Outcomes lag. They bounce around. They depend on timing in other people's lives that you can't control. But conversations started is something you can see, week by week. In episode fifty-one, we'll go deeper into tracking. For now, just start counting.

[pause]

Let me give you a picture of how this can look.

Picture an adviser who has spoken to almost everyone in her phone in her first year. She feels the pull to go round again. To call the same friends a second time, with a slightly different angle.

Instead, she stops and picks two channels she can sustain.

The first is a monthly small-group talk on a money-and-life topic, for a hobby community she already belongs to. People sign up knowing an adviser is speaking. Nobody is surprised. Nobody feels trapped.

The second is a short weekly note to people who have said yes to hearing from her. Something useful. Something short.

She blocks two hours a week for each one. And she counts conversations started, not sales.

After three months, the pipeline is slower than she hoped. I want to be honest about that. It's not a miracle. But it's steadier. She knows where next month's conversations are likely to come from. And nobody in her family has had a second awkward call.

That last part matters more than it sounds. She still gets to be a daughter, a sister, a friend, at the dinner table. Her work and her relationships aren't fighting each other anymore.

[pause]

So, what can you do this week? Three things.

First. Look back at the last three months. List every place your new conversations came from. Be honest. Write them all down, even the ones that felt uncomfortable. You might be surprised by what's actually working, and by how much is still coming from one shrinking list.

Second. Circle the two sources you could repeat every week without discomfort. Not the ones that look impressive. The ones you could keep doing for a year without dreading them. Then put one recurring block for each into your calendar. Protect those blocks the way you'd protect a client meeting.

Third. Before you use any new channel, ask your compliance team what applies. Especially for anything in public places, phone calls or text messages. The MAS changes I mentioned are still being finalised, so check what applies to you now, and what will apply once the new guidelines are issued.

That's it. Three steps. Nothing dramatic. Just a little structure, repeated.

[pause]

Next episode, on The Resilient Advisor track, we'll look at the other side of this. The fear of becoming "that person" at gatherings. The one everyone quietly avoids. Because a system helps, but how you feel about reaching out matters just as much.

Here's the line I'd like you to leave with.

Your warm list got you started. A steady, honest system is what lets you keep going, and keep your friends.

Thanks for listening to Quiet Compounding. I'm Jason Ng. Take care of your clients, take care of yourself, and I'll see you next time.
