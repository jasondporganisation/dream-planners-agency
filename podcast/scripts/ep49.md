## Script

You've just finished a good review. The client is relaxed. They said it was useful. And there's a moment where you know you could ask for a referral.

But you don't. Because asking feels like begging. It feels like you're turning a good conversation into a sales pitch. So you smile, you pack up, and you tell yourself you'll ask next time.

If that sounds familiar, this episode is for you.

[pause]

I'm Jason Ng, and this is Quiet Compounding. This is a show for financial advisory professionals in Singapore about building the practice, and the person behind it. Everything here is my personal view as a representative, I'm not speaking on behalf of any company, and it's general. Your firm's rules come first.

This is an Agency Blueprint episode, so we're talking about systems. And today's question is a simple one. How do you make referrals a normal part of your work, without making clients uncomfortable, and without making yourself feel small?

Here's the one idea I want you to take away. A referral is not a favour you ask for. It's a habit you build into your process.

Let me unpack that.

Start with what a referral actually is. It's a client choosing to share something that helped them with someone they care about. That's it. Your job is not to pull names out of them. Your job is to make that sharing easy, and safe, for everyone involved.

When you see it that way, a few things change.

The first thing that changes is timing. If you only ask for referrals when your diary is empty, the ask carries all your worry with it. Clients can feel that. It comes out rushed, or apologetic, or both. But if the question sits at a fixed point in your process, it stops being a big moment. It's just part of how you work. Something like the end of a review, when the client has told you the meeting was useful. That's a natural place. You're not interrupting anything. You're closing well.

The second thing that changes is what you ask for. Don't ask for a list. Ask for an introduction. The cleanest route goes like this. Your client mentions you to their friend first. The friend then contacts you, or agrees to be contacted. Nobody is surprised. Nobody gets a call out of the blue.

And this isn't just good manners. It lines up with how personal data rules work here.

The Personal Data Protection Commission has guidance on this. In its advisory guidelines on key concepts in the data protection act, revised in April twenty twenty-six, the Commission says organisations may collect personal data from a third party with the person's consent. And it actually gives a referral from an existing customer as an example. But it also says you should do some checking that the source could validly give that consent, or had obtained it. For example, by written confirmation, or by documented verbal confirmation.

The same guidelines walk through a worked example of a referral programme. Before recording the friend's details, the staff member asks the referrer whether the friend was told what the details are for, and whether the friend agreed. And as good practice, the guidelines say that when the organisation first contacts the friend, it should tell them who passed on their details, and check that they agreed.

So in plain words. Before you take a friend's number, ask your client: did they say it's okay? When you call, open with who referred you, and check the friend is comfortable. Write down what was agreed. That protects the friend, it protects your client, and it protects you.

[pause]

Now, there's a second rule I want to flag, because it trips people up. Paid introducer arrangements are a different thing altogether, and they are regulated.

In May twenty twenty-six, the Monetary Authority of Singapore published a response to feedback on digital prospecting and marketing safeguards. In it, MAS pointed to its Notice on the appointment and use of introducers, and said that only financial institutions may appoint introducers. Employees or representatives are not permitted to enter into their own arrangements with introducers to do introducing work on their behalf.

In the same response, MAS said financial institutions should have written agreements with introducers covering how those introducers collect, share and dispose of people's data. And MAS said it would give a nine-month transition from when the updated rules are issued.

Those updated rules may or may not be out by the time you hear this. So please check with your compliance team on where things stand.

What does this mean for you, day to day? It means you don't set up your own deal where someone sends you names in return for something. Not a voucher, not cash, not a gift for every name. If you're thinking of offering anyone anything connected to a referral, stop and check with your compliance team first.

And one more honest note. I haven't looked into whether a client's informal, unpaid mention of you counts as introducing activity under MAS rules. I'm not going to guess on air. If you're unsure, check with your compliance team.

What you can do is say thank you. A short, sincere thank-you note, with no strings. If you want to go beyond a note, check your firm's rules on gifts first.

[pause]

So we have the reframe, the timing, the introduction, the data step, and the line you don't cross. The last piece is patience. Because this is a habit, and habits take time.

There's a study from twenty ten, published in the European Journal of Social Psychology and summarised by the British Psychological Society's Research Digest. Ninety-six people set out to repeat a new daily behaviour, and eighty-two saw it through. On average, it took about sixty-six days for the behaviour to feel as automatic as it was going to get. For some people it was as quick as eighteen days. For others, the researchers predicted it would take up to two hundred and fifty-four days.

Now, these were students working on health habits, not advisers. So I'm not saying your referral habit will take sixty-six days. What I take from it is simpler. A new routine takes a while to feel natural. And here's the useful part. The study found that missing a single day had little effect on later progress.

So if you forget to ask at one review, that's fine. Ask at the next one.

[pause]

Let me make this concrete.

Picture an adviser who used to ask for referrals only when her diary was empty. And every time, it came out sounding a bit desperate. She hated it, so she mostly didn't do it.

Then she changes one thing. At the end of every annual review, if the client says it was useful, she asks one question. "Is there anyone you think would value a conversation like this? If so, would you mention me to them first, and let me know if they're happy to hear from me?"

That's all. No pressure. No list.

She keeps a simple log. Who said yes, and what the friend was told. When a friend agrees to hear from her, she calls, and she opens with who referred her. Then she checks the friend is comfortable talking.

Some months, nothing comes of it. That's okay. The question still gets asked, at every review. And over time it stops feeling like a favour. It just feels like part of doing a good review.

Notice what she didn't do. She didn't wait for courage. She didn't chase names. She tied one small question to one fixed point, and she kept going.

[pause]

So here are three things to do this week.

First, choose one fixed point in your client process where a referral question fits naturally. The end of a review is a good candidate. Pick one, and only one.

Second, write down the exact words you'll use. Make sure they include the "ask them first" step, so your client checks with their friend before passing anything on. Then run those words past your compliance team.

Third, once your team has checked it, use that question at every matching meeting for the next month. Keep a short note of who agreed and what their friend was told. And if you miss one, don't worry. Just ask at the next.

[pause]

Here's the line I'd like you to keep.

A referral isn't something you take from a client. It's something you make easy for them to give.

Next episode is the companion to this one, on The Resilient Advisor track. We'll talk about what a "no" does to you, and what to do with it. Because when you ask more often, you'll hear more noes, and that deserves its own conversation.

Thanks for listening to Quiet Compounding. I'm Jason Ng. Take care, and I'll see you next time.
