## Script

Let me describe a scene. It's April. Tax season. And on the dining table there's a shoebox. Inside it, a year of receipts. Some faded. Some crumpled. Some, you're fairly sure, are missing altogether. And you're sitting there, trying to remember what that parking receipt from last July was for.

If you've ever had that evening, you're not alone. When you work for yourself, nobody deducts anything for you. Nobody files it for you. Nobody keeps the paperwork for you. It's all on you. And it's easy to put off until it becomes a problem.

So today, let's make it simple. What you have to file. What you have to pay. And what you have to keep.

I'm Jason Ng, and this is Quiet Compounding.

[pause]

Before we start, an important note. This is general information only. It is not tax advice. Rules change, so please check the IRAS and CPF Board websites yourself. And for your own case, speak to IRAS, CPF Board, or a qualified tax professional. Whether you count as self-employed for tax purposes depends on your own arrangement, so check that too.

[pause]

We're near the end of our second theme, the working week and your energy. Two episodes ago, we talked about money habits, paying yourself a salary, and setting money aside for tax. Today we go one step further, into the paperwork side.

Here's the one idea I'd like you to take away. Treat your records as a weekly habit, not a yearly panic. A few minutes every week saves you a very long evening in April, and it protects you if anyone ever asks questions.

[pause]

Let's start with filing.

IRAS lists commission agents among self-employed individuals. And for commission agents, IRAS says you must declare your commission and any other self-employment income in your tax return.

There's a detail here that catches people out. Sometimes the organisation paying you will also send your figures to IRAS, and they may appear pre-filled in your return. IRAS says you still need to check those figures, and amend them if they're wrong. Pre-filled doesn't mean you can skip looking. It's still your return.

Broadly, your taxable business income is your income, less your allowable business expenses. I'm not going to tell you what you can or can't claim. That depends on your situation, and it's a question for IRAS or a tax professional. But notice what that sentence means. If you want expenses to count, you need to be able to show them. And that brings us to records.

[pause]

So, what do you have to keep?

IRAS says self-employed individuals must keep proper records and accounts of their business transactions for five years. Those records should be supported by things like invoices, receipts, vouchers, and other documents.

And there's one line on the IRAS page I want you to hear clearly. It says, "Estimates and improper records are not acceptable."

So that shoebox approach, where you piece together a year in April and fill the gaps with guesses? That's exactly what IRAS is warning about.

What happens if records are missing? IRAS says it may disallow expenses, assess extra income based on its best estimate, and, or, impose penalties.

Now, the good news. IRAS also says you don't need to submit these records with your return unless they ask for them. So this isn't about sending boxes of paper anywhere. It's about having them ready, and in order, for five years, in case you're asked.

[pause]

Now let's talk about MediSave.

This one surprises some newer advisers. CPF Board says that if your annual net trade income, or NTI, is more than six thousand dollars, you're required to contribute to your MediSave Account as a self-employed person.

So what's net trade income? CPF Board explains it as your gross trade income, minus allowable business expenses, capital allowances and trade losses, as determined by IRAS. Which, once again, is why your records matter. They feed into the number that decides what you owe.

How does it actually work? After IRAS completes your tax assessment, CPF Board sends you a notice of the contributions you need to make as a self-employed person. Payment is due within thirty days of that notice. And CPF Board says you can ask for an instalment plan if you need one.

How much will it be? It depends on your age and your NTI. I'm not going to quote rates on air, because the right figure depends on your situation, and the official calculator will always be more up to date than I am. CPF Board has a calculator on its website. Use that.

[pause]

So let's put that together into a simple picture of the year.

The income comes in. You keep records as you go. You file your return and check the pre-filled figures. IRAS assesses your tax. Then the tax bill arrives, and the MediSave notice from CPF Board arrives too, with a thirty-day clock.

Both of those bills arrive months after the income that created them. That's why, two episodes ago, I suggested setting money aside for tax and MediSave as the income comes in. So that when those letters arrive, they're not a shock.

[pause]

Let me give you a picture of one adviser.

Picture an adviser who keeps her receipts in a shoebox and pieces them together every April. It's stressful, but she gets through it. Then one year, she can't find proof for several expenses. She's quite sure she spent the money on her work. But she can't show it. So she leaves them out.

That was the year she changed her approach.

Now, every Friday, in her weekly review, the one we talked about a few episodes ago, she takes five minutes. She photographs that week's receipts into one folder, with a sub-folder for each month. She adds a line for each one to a simple expense sheet. Date, amount, what it was for. That's all.

And she keeps a small reminder in her calendar. Keep records for five years before deleting anything.

When her CPF notice arrives after her tax assessment, she's ready. She's already set money aside. She knows the thirty-day deadline. And April is just another month.

[pause]

A few honest cautions before we finish.

Don't take tax shortcuts from colleagues, or from anything you hear on a podcast, including this one. What one person can claim may not apply to you. If you're not sure, ask IRAS, or a tax professional.

Don't rely on estimates. If something matters enough to claim, it matters enough to keep the receipt.

Don't mix things up. If your business receipts and your personal receipts all live in the same pile, sorting them later takes far longer. One folder, just for business, keeps it clean.

And if you lead newer advisers, tell them about this early. In their first year, nobody may have explained that the tax bill and the MediSave notice come later. A five-minute conversation now can spare them a very stressful letter next year.

And don't wait for the notice to think about MediSave. If your NTI is likely to be above the threshold, plan for it during the year.

[pause]

So here are three things to do this week.

First, set up one folder for this year's records, paper or digital, with twelve monthly sub-folders inside it. Simple is fine. What matters is that there's one place.

Second, go back through the past month and put every business receipt you can find into that folder. And add a line for each to a simple expense sheet.

Third, add two reminders to your calendar. One every Friday: file this week's receipts. And one long-term reminder: keep records for five years before deleting anything. While you're at it, look up CPF Board's calculator, so you have a rough idea of what to set aside.

[pause]

Here's what I'd like you to carry this week. Records aren't paperwork for the sake of it. They're how you show your work, to IRAS, and to yourself. Five minutes a week beats one long night in April.

Next time, we close this second theme with something that's harder than any tax return for many of us. Asking for help.

Thanks for listening to Quiet Compounding. These are my personal views as a representative, and I'm not speaking on behalf of any company. It's general information, not tax advice, and not advice for your situation. Please check with IRAS, CPF Board, or a qualified professional. Take care, and I'll see you next time.
