## Script

Here's a pattern I know well. Maybe you do too.

Sunday night, you decide this is the week. You'll write up your client notes every single day. You'll make your follow-up calls first thing. You'll read for fifteen minutes before bed. And Monday goes well. Tuesday goes well. By Thursday, a meeting runs late, you skip one day, and then somehow the whole thing just falls away. By the next Sunday, you're back where you started, and a little voice says: you just don't have the discipline.

I want to push back on that voice today. Because the research on habits tells a different, kinder story.

I'm Jason Ng, and this is Quiet Compounding.

[pause]

We're in our second theme, the working week and your energy. Last time, we talked about time-blocking, giving every kind of work a home in your week. Today we go underneath that. Because a calendar is only a plan. What actually carries you through a busy week is habit.

Here's the one idea I want you to take away. A habit is not built on willpower. It's built on a cue and repetition. When a familiar situation comes up, and you've done the same thing in that situation enough times, the situation starts to do the work for you.

[pause]

Let's start with what a habit actually is.

Two psychologists, Wendy Wood and David Neal, wrote about this in two thousand and seven. They described habits as links that build up gradually between an action and features of the situation around it. Things like a physical place, or something you've just done. Once those links are formed, the situation triggers the action, without a goal having to step in first. And they noted that these links build slowly.

A later review by Wood and a colleague, in two thousand and sixteen, put it another way. People form habits as they pursue goals, by repeating the same responses in the same context. And once formed, habits become the efficient, default way we respond.

So think about what that means. Goals still matter. Your goal is what gets you to repeat the action in the first place. But the thing that eventually carries the habit is the cue. The same time. The same place. The same thing you did just before.

That's why "I'll write my notes sometime each day" rarely sticks. There's no cue. There's no "when". It depends on you remembering, and choosing, every single time. That's the willpower trap.

[pause]

Now, how long does it take?

You've probably heard twenty-one days. It's everywhere. I'd like you to let that one go, because the research doesn't support it.

The study that's often quoted was done at University College London, published in two thousand and ten, led by Dr Lally. Ninety-six volunteers each chose a simple eating, drinking or activity behaviour to do daily, in the same context, over twelve weeks.

The researchers tracked how automatic the behaviour felt over time. And the time it took people to get close to their peak level of automaticity ranged from eighteen days to two hundred and fifty-four days. That's a huge range. You'll often hear sixty-six days quoted from this study. In a twenty twenty-six interview with the University of Surrey, Dr Lally said the average in her study was sixty-six days for a daily habit. But she also said it's not a general rule, it's often taken out of context, and habit formation time is highly variable.

So the honest version is this. In one study, it took about sixty-six days on average, and it varied a lot from person to person. Some of those longer times were estimated from the trend, not watched all the way. And these were simple health behaviours, not advisers building work routines. So please don't treat sixty-six days as a deadline. If your habit takes longer, you're not failing. You're normal.

[pause]

And here's the part of that study I find most freeing.

Missing one opportunity did not materially affect habit formation. One missed day did not send people back to the start.

Think about that Thursday from the start of this episode. The meeting ran late. You missed one day. And because of the story we tell ourselves, one missed day turned into giving up. But the research suggests the missed day itself wasn't the problem. The giving up was.

So the rule I'd like you to try is simple. If you miss once, just do it again at the next chance. No guilt. No restarting the count. No big speech to yourself. Just the next time.

[pause]

Let me give you a picture of how this can look.

Picture an adviser who wants to update her client notes after every meeting. She knows it matters. She knows a fresh note is better than one written days later from memory. But she keeps forgetting. By the end of the week, there's a pile, and it feels heavy.

So she stops relying on willpower. She ties the habit to a cue that already happens. "When I sit back in the car after a meeting, I open my notes and write three lines." That's it. Three lines. Not the full write-up. Just enough to capture what matters while it's fresh.

Some weeks she misses one. A call comes in, or she's running late. She doesn't restart from zero. She doesn't decide she's bad at this. She just does it after the next meeting.

A couple of months in, something shifts. It starts to feel odd to drive off without writing. The car has become the cue. She isn't forcing herself any more. The situation reminds her.

Nothing heroic happened. She made the cue obvious, kept the action small, and kept going after slips.

And notice what she didn't do. She didn't wait until she felt motivated. She didn't buy a new notebook and promise herself a fresh start on the first of the month. She didn't try to change five things at once. She picked one moment in her day that was already happening, and she attached one small action to it. That's the whole method. It's almost boring. But boring and repeated is exactly how a habit grows.

[pause]

A couple of things that help.

Make the cue obvious. Tie the new habit to something you already do every day. After my first coffee. When I sit down at my desk. When I close a client meeting. The clearer the "when", the less you have to remember.

Make the action small. Smaller than you think you need. Three lines of notes, not a full write-up. One follow-up call, not ten. You can grow it later, once the habit has a foothold. Small and repeated beats big and abandoned.

And be patient with yourself. The study showed big differences between people. Your colleague might settle into a routine in a few weeks. It might take you a few months. That tells you nothing about your worth, or your future in this work. It's just how habits are.

[pause]

So here are three things to do this week.

First, choose one small work habit. Just one. Write it in this form: "After I do this existing routine, I will do this tiny action." For example, "After I end a client meeting, I will write three lines of notes."

Second, do it in the same place, or after the same thing, each time. Same context. That's what lets the habit build.

Third, put a small tick on a calendar each day you do it. And if you miss a day, leave it blank and carry on. Don't start the count again. Don't make it mean anything. Just tick the next one.

[pause]

One more thought before we close. If you lead a team, or you're thinking about it one day, this matters for how you talk to others too. When someone drops a routine, "you need more discipline" is rarely the helpful answer. "What's the cue?" usually is.

Here's what I'd like you to carry this week. You are not short of discipline. You're short of a cue. Find the cue, keep it small, and forgive the missed days.

Next time, we'll put this to work with one habit that holds a whole week together. The weekly review. A short meeting with yourself that stops promises slipping through the cracks.

Thanks for listening to Quiet Compounding. These are my personal views as a representative, and I'm not speaking on behalf of any company. It's general discussion, not advice for your situation. This episode was checked by my team. Be kind to yourself this week, and I'll see you next time.
