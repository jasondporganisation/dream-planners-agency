## Script

It's the last week of the month. You open your summary, and the numbers are thin. Again.

And the thought that follows is a familiar one. If only I'd known three weeks ago.

Here's the honest answer. You could have known. Not from the month-end summary. That number was going to arrive late either way. But from a few small numbers you could have looked at every Friday.

[pause]

I'm Jason Ng, and this is Quiet Compounding. A show for financial advisers in Singapore about building a practice, and the person behind it, one steady habit at a time.

This is an Agency Blueprint episode. That's the track about systems, numbers and structure. And today's question comes up a lot in conversations with advisers. It sounds like this. "I only look at my results at month-end, and by then it's too late. What should I actually be tracking week to week?"

Let's work through it.

Start with two words. Lagging and leading.

A lagging number tells you what already happened. New clients onboarded. Reviews completed. Plans put in place. These matter. They're the outcome. But by the time you see them, the work that produced them is weeks old. You can read them. You can't steer by them.

A leading number is an activity you control that comes before the outcome. Conversations started. First meetings held. Follow-ups done when you said you'd do them. These happen this week. You can see them this week. And you can change them this week.

Here's the problem with living on lagging numbers alone. They arrive late. And they depend on a lot of things outside your hands. A client travels. A family has a health scare. Someone needs another month to think. None of that is a failure on your part. But if month-end is the only number you look at, it all lands on you at once, with no time left to respond.

Leading numbers give you something different. They give you a lever. You can't decide when a client says yes. You can decide whether you called back on Tuesday like you promised.

Think of it like driving at night. The lagging number is the road sign you've just passed. It tells you where you were. The leading numbers are your headlights. They only show a short stretch ahead. But that short stretch is exactly the part you can still do something about.

And there's a quieter benefit too. When you only see month-end, a thin month can feel like a mystery. Something that just happened to you. When you've been watching your weekly activity, it's rarely a mystery. You can usually point to the weeks where things changed. That takes a lot of the anxiety out of it, because a problem you can see is a problem you can work on.

[pause]

So how do you pick them?

Keep it small. Three or four leading numbers. No more. A long list is easy to drop by week three. A short list is one you're more likely to keep.

And choose numbers that link to good client outcomes, not just volume. "Conversations started" is useful. "Follow-ups done when promised" is useful, because it's about keeping your word to people. A number that only rewards being busy is less useful.

Then write them down, and look at them every week.

There's research behind this part, and I want to describe it carefully. In twenty sixteen, a team of psychologists led by Harkin published a meta-analysis in the journal Psychological Bulletin. A meta-analysis pools the results of many studies. This one brought together one hundred and thirty-eight randomised studies, with nearly twenty thousand participants in total. They asked a simple question. When people are prompted to monitor their progress towards a goal, are they more likely to reach it?

They found that, on average, yes. Prompting people to monitor their progress helped them reach their goals. The average effect was modest to moderate. Not magic. But real. And how often people checked their progress was part of how the effect worked.

Two more details from that same study are worth knowing. The effects were larger when progress was physically recorded, written down rather than just kept in your head. And they were larger when progress was reported to someone else, or made public.

Now, a fair caveat. That analysis looked at goal pursuit in general. Many kinds of goals, many kinds of people. It wasn't a study of advisers. So I'm not telling you it proves anything about our industry. I'm telling you it's a reasonable basis for a habit. Write it down. Look at it often. Share it with someone you trust.

There's also a long line of research on goal-setting itself. Back in two thousand and two, the researchers Locke and Latham published a review in the American Psychologist that summarised thirty-five years of studies on how goals affect what people do, including the limits of the theory. I won't quote numbers from it here. The practical point I take from that body of work is simple. A vague intention like "do more this week" is hard to act on. A clear weekly target for an activity you control is easier to plan around, and easier to review honestly.

[pause]

Now, there's a trap here, and I want to name it.

When a count becomes the goal, quality can slip. If your number is "first meetings held", it's tempting to rush them. To book anyone, whether or not it's a good fit. To end a meeting without a clear next step, because the count went up either way.

That's not what we're building. So alongside your three or four activity numbers, keep one quality check. Something like, "Did each client leave knowing the next step?" Or, "Did I do what I said I would, when I said I would?" It's a yes or no. And it keeps the counting honest.

And one more thing. Review, don't judge.

A bad week of numbers is information. It's not a verdict on you. If your follow-ups dropped, that tells you where to look. It doesn't tell you you're not good enough. In the next episode, episode fifty-two, on The Resilient Advisor track, we'll go deeper into exactly that. How you talk to yourself after a bad week, and why it matters.

[pause]

Let me make this concrete.

Picture an adviser who only ever looked at her month-end summary. And every month, the last week turned into a scramble. Late nights. Chasing. Feeling behind.

So she tries something simple. She draws a card with three weekly counts. New conversations started. First meetings held. Follow-ups done when promised. Underneath, she adds one quality question. "Did each client leave knowing the next step?"

Every Friday afternoon, she fills it in by hand. Ten minutes. Then she sends a photo of it to an accountability partner, a colleague she trusts, who does the same.

For the first couple of weeks, nothing dramatic happens. It just feels like admin. But after a few weeks, a pattern shows up. Her quiet months follow the weeks where her follow-ups slipped. Not the weeks where she started fewer conversations. The weeks where she said, "I'll get back to you on Thursday," and then didn't.

That changes what she works on. She doesn't rewrite how she explains things in meetings. She doesn't push harder on anyone. She fixes her follow-up. She puts each promised follow-up into her calendar before she leaves the meeting. That's it.

That's the value of a leading number. It shows you where the leak is, while there's still time to fix it.

[pause]

So here's what to do this week. Three things.

First, choose three leading numbers you fully control. Activities, not outcomes. Things like conversations started, first meetings held, or follow-ups done when promised. Pick the ones that link to good client experiences, not just busyness. And add one quality check, a simple yes or no question you'll answer honestly.

Second, draw a simple weekly grid. Paper is fine. A note on your phone is fine. Three rows for your numbers, one row for your quality check, and four columns for the next four weeks. The research we talked about found bigger effects when progress was physically recorded, so actually write it down. Don't keep it in your head.

Third, fill it in every Friday for four weeks, and show it to one person. A trusted colleague, a mentor, someone in your team. Not to be judged. Just to have someone who sees it. At the end of the four weeks, sit down together and ask one question. What is this telling me?

That's it. No new software. No big targets. Just three small numbers, one honest question, and a Friday habit.

[pause]

If you only remember one line from today, let it be this. Month-end tells you what happened; Friday tells you what to do next.

Thanks for listening to Quiet Compounding. These are my personal views, shared for advisers, and I'm not speaking on behalf of any company. The studies I mentioned are listed in the show notes, so you can read them for yourself. Next time, on The Resilient Advisor, we'll talk about the voice in your head after a bad week.

Until then, keep it quiet, and keep it compounding.
