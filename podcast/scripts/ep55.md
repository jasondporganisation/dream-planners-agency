## Script

Be honest with me for a second. Where do your contacts actually live?

If you're honest, the answer might be: everywhere. An old spreadsheet from your first year. Notes on your phone. A notebook in your bag. Chat threads going back years, with someone's new number buried halfway down. And somewhere in all of that, a quiet worry. What am I actually supposed to be doing with all these people's details?

That's today's question. Not the legal deep end. Just the plain, practical version. What does looking after people's personal data look like, week to week, in a real advisory practice?

I'm Jason Ng, and this is Quiet Compounding.

[pause]

This episode closes our first theme, a pipeline without pressure. We've talked about prospecting, referrals, follow-up and patience. And all of it rests on one thing we rarely talk about. The list itself.

So here's the one idea I want you to leave with today. Your contact list is not really yours. It's other people's personal data, which they have trusted you with. Once you see it that way, a lot of decisions get easier.

In Singapore, the Personal Data Protection Act, the PDPA, sets out obligations for how personal data is collected, used, kept accurate, protected, and eventually disposed of. The Personal Data Protection Commission, the PDPC, publishes guidelines explaining those obligations. The version I'm drawing on today, their Advisory Guidelines on Key Concepts, was revised in April twenty twenty-six.

One thing before we go on. The law talks about obligations on "organisations". How those duties split between you as a representative and the firm you work with is something your firm sets out. So when I say "you" today, I mean you and your firm together. And on top of the law, your firm will have its own rules. Follow them. They come first in your day-to-day.

Let's walk through four ideas from the guidelines, and then one about what happens when things go wrong.

The first is purpose. The PDPC's guidelines say an organisation may collect, use or disclose personal data only for purposes a reasonable person would consider appropriate in the circumstances, and, where it applies, that the person has been told about. In plain words: the reason you collected something matters. A note you took during a review meeting is not automatically fine to use for a marketing message. Ask yourself, would this person expect me to use their details this way? If you'd feel awkward explaining it to them, that's your answer.

The second is accuracy. The guidelines say an organisation must make a reasonable effort to make sure personal data is accurate and complete if it's likely to be used to make a decision affecting the person, or passed to another organisation. That includes recording what you collect correctly in the first place. Think about what that means for us. Three versions of a phone number. Two dates of birth. An address from before they moved. Messy records are not just untidy. They can lead to wrong decisions about real people.

The third is protection. The guidelines say an organisation must make reasonable security arrangements to protect the personal data it holds, against things like unauthorised access, copying, changes or disposal, and against losing the devices it's stored on. And here's the part I find helpful. The PDPC says there is no one size fits all solution. So don't look for a magic setting. Think about what kind of data it is, and how much harm it could cause if it got out. A phone with client details on it, left unlocked on a café table, is a very different risk from a printed event flyer.

The fourth is retention. This one is worth slowing down for. The guidelines say an organisation should stop keeping documents with personal data, or anonymise them, once it's reasonable to assume the purpose is no longer being served and there's no legal or business need to keep them. The PDPA doesn't set one fixed period. Your firm sets its own retention periods. But the principle is clear. Not forever. Not "just in case". If you've got a spreadsheet of people you met at an event years ago, sitting on your personal laptop, that's worth a hard look.

[pause]

Now, what happens when something goes wrong? Say a phone is lost, or a list gets sent to the wrong person.

The PDPC's Guide on Managing and Notifying Data Breaches, revised in March twenty twenty-one, sets out some clocks. Once there are credible grounds to believe a breach has happened, an organisation must take reasonable and quick steps to assess whether it's notifiable, within thirty calendar days. If it decides the breach is notifiable, it must tell the PDPC as soon as practicable, and no later than three calendar days. The same guide says a breach affecting the personal data of five hundred or more people is treated as significant in scale, and must be notified to the PDPC.

You don't need to memorise all of that. What you need to remember is this. Those clocks are short, and they start early. So if you think something has gone wrong, tell your firm straight away. Don't sit on it. Don't try to work out on your own whether it's "big enough". A small leak still goes to your firm, today. That's how your firm can run its process properly.

[pause]

So how does this turn into something you actually do? Let me give you a picture.

Picture an adviser who finally sits down to look at his contacts. He finds three versions of the same person. One in an old spreadsheet, one in his phone notes, one in a chat thread. Each has a different number. Then he notices something worse. One person asked him last year not to be contacted again, and that request only lives in the chat. Nowhere else.

So he blocks out one afternoon. He moves everything into the system his firm approves. He marks that "do not contact" request clearly, so he won't miss it again. He records when and how each person agreed to hear from him. For old records, he doesn't just keep them in case. He flags them for his firm's retention process. He deletes the stray spreadsheet from his personal laptop, following his firm's process for that. And then he sets a thirty-minute reminder, once a month, to tidy up.

Nothing dramatic happened there. No big software project. No weekend lost. But look at what changed. He knows where his records are. He knows who has said no. And the next time he picks up the phone, he's calling someone who expects to hear from him. That's a pipeline without pressure. Not just less pressure on the other person. Less on him, too.

That's the shift I want you to notice. Hygiene is a routine, not a project. One place for records. Consent and "do not contact" flags written down. A short tidy-up each month. And no client data in personal chat groups or apps your firm hasn't allowed.

[pause]

So here are three things to do this week.

First, make a list of every place you keep client or prospect details. Your phone. Your laptop. Chat apps. Notebooks. Old email folders. Screenshots. Write them all down on one page.

Second, take that list to your compliance team and ask which of those places are approved. Then, following your firm's process, move what should be moved and remove what should be removed. And while you're in there, look for any "please don't contact me" requests that only live in a chat or a note. Put them where you'll actually see them.

Third, book a recurring thirty minutes in your calendar, once a month. Call it your data tidy-up. Fix duplicates. Update details you know have changed. Flag old records for your firm's retention process. Small, regular, boring. That's the point.

[pause]

As we close this first theme, I want to bring it back to where we started. Everything we've talked about, from prospecting to follow-up to patience, has been about earning trust slowly. Looking after someone's details is the quietest form of that. They'll probably never know you did it. But you will.

Here's the line I'd like you to carry this week: treat every name in your list as a person who trusted you, not a number you collected.

Next time, we start a new theme, the working week and your energy. And we'll begin with a word that can be hard to say out loud. Burnout. What it is, and how to spot the early signs.

Thanks for listening to Quiet Compounding. This episode was checked by my team. Take care of yourself, and take care of your list. I'll see you next time.
