## Script

Think about your job for a moment. When something goes wrong in a client's life, who do they call? Often, it's you. When a family is facing a hard decision, who sits with them and helps them think it through? You. When a newer colleague is stuck, who do they come to? Very possibly, you.

We spend our days being the steady one. And that's a good thing. It's part of why people trust us.

But here's the question I want to ask today. When you're the one who's struggling, who do you call?

I'm Jason Ng, and this is Quiet Compounding.

[pause]

Before anything else, I want to give you some numbers, right at the start, in case you need them now. You don't have to wait for the end of this episode.

National Mindline is open twenty-four hours. You can call one seven seven one. You can WhatsApp plus six five, six six six nine, one seven seven one. Or you can use the webchat at mindline dot s g. And you can stay anonymous if you'd like.

The Samaritans of Singapore have a twenty-four hour hotline on one seven six seven, and a twenty-four hour CareText service on WhatsApp, at nine one five one, one seven six seven.

And if you or someone else is in immediate danger, call nine nine five, or go to the nearest A and E.

I'm not a doctor, and this episode isn't medical advice. But I'd like to talk honestly about why asking is hard, and how to make it a little easier.

[pause]

This is the last episode in our second theme, the working week and your energy. We started with burnout. We've talked about time, habits, sleep, money, comparison, and records. And I wanted to end the theme here, because none of those tools work very well if you're carrying something heavy on your own.

Here's the one idea I'd like you to take away. Asking for help is not a sign you've failed. It's a skill. And like any skill, it gets easier with practice, and it works best when you use it early.

[pause]

Let's start with the fact that you're not alone in finding this hard.

The Singapore Mental Health Study in twenty sixteen surveyed more than six thousand adults here. The researchers found that thirteen point nine per cent had experienced at least one mood, anxiety, or alcohol use disorder at some point in their lives.

And a follow-up paper from the same study looked at what's called the treatment gap. That means people who had a mental health condition in the past year, but hadn't received treatment for it. That gap was seventy-eight point six per cent. In other words, most people with a condition in the past year had not got treatment.

But here's the finding that stopped me. The same research found that people with post-secondary education, and people who were employed, were more likely to have a treatment gap. Not less likely. More. The authors called for more support in education and workplace settings.

I want to be careful. That's research on adults in Singapore generally, not on advisers. So I won't claim anything about advisers in particular. But I do think it's worth sitting with. Being educated, being in work, being capable, doesn't make us more likely to get help. If anything, that study suggests the opposite.

[pause]

So why is it so hard for people like us?

I think part of it is the role. When you're the steady one all day, admitting you're not steady can feel like letting people down. Part of it might be a quiet voice that says, "I help other people. I shouldn't need help myself." And for those of us who lead teams, there's often another voice. "Leaders should cope. If I fall apart, what happens to everyone else?"

And there's something else specific to our work. We're used to fixing things. A client has a problem, we find a way through. So when we're the one with the problem, we often try to fix it alone, the same way. We make a plan. We push harder. We tell ourselves we'll sort it out next week. But some things aren't solved by working harder. They're eased by sharing them.

I'd like to gently challenge all of those voices. Strong people do need help. Good leaders do ask. The people you most admire have almost certainly leaned on someone at some point. You just didn't see it.

[pause]

It can help to think of help as coming in layers. You don't have to jump straight to the biggest step.

The first layer might be a trusted friend, or a leader you respect. Someone you can just say "I'm not okay" to.

The next might be a peer or a mentor. Someone who understands this work, and its particular pressures.

Then there's your GP. A doctor is a good person to talk to if you're not sleeping, not eating, or just not feeling like yourself for a while. Talking to a friend is valuable, but it's not a substitute for a doctor when you need one.

There are counsellors and other professionals.

And there are national support lines like Mindline and the Samaritans, which are there any hour of the day.

Singapore now has a single round-the-clock starting point. MOH announced National Mindline one seven seven one in June twenty twenty-five, as the country's first national mental health helpline and textline. And in April twenty twenty-six, MOH said it had received more than forty-seven thousand calls, messages and webchats since it started. That tells me something. A lot of people are reaching out. You wouldn't be the only one.

And one more thing. You don't have to be in crisis to call. Asking early is usually easier than asking late.

[pause]

Let me give you a picture.

Picture an agency leader who's the first person his team calls when a case goes wrong. He's good at it. Calm, practical, reassuring. But it's been a hard few months at home. He's sleeping badly. He's started snapping at people, which isn't like him. And he keeps telling himself the same thing. Leaders should cope.

One evening, late, he sends a WhatsApp message to Mindline. He doesn't give his name. He just wants to talk it through with someone who isn't part of his work or his family. And that conversation gives him a small push.

That week, he books an appointment with his GP. He tells a peer leader, someone he trusts, what's been going on. And he does one more thing. He tells his team he's taking a Friday afternoon off, for himself.

He doesn't make a big speech about it. But his team notices. And quietly, without him saying so, it gives them permission to look after themselves too.

I can't tell you how things turned out for him, and I won't promise that one call fixes anything. That's not how it works. But asking was the first step. And he took it.

[pause]

If you lead others, I'd ask you to think about that last part. Your team watches how you handle hard times. If you push through alone, they'll learn to do the same. If you ask for help, and talk about it in a simple, matter-of-fact way, you make it easier for them to ask too.

[pause]

So here are three things to do this week.

First, save the numbers in your phone now. National Mindline, one seven seven one. Samaritans of Singapore, one seven six seven. It takes thirty seconds, and it means they're there if you, or someone you know, ever needs them.

Second, write down the names of two people you could call if you had a really bad week. Just two.

Third, send one of them a short message this week. Not because anything is wrong. Just to stay in touch. If you are struggling, you can use a line as simple as this: "I've not been myself lately. Can we talk this week?" That's enough.

[pause]

Here's what I'd like you to carry this week. You spend your days being someone others can lean on. You're allowed to lean too.

That closes our second theme. Next time, we begin a new one: compliance, ethics, and hard conversations. And we'll start with an idea that might sound dull, but I think is quietly powerful. Compliance as a daily habit.

Thanks for listening to Quiet Compounding. These are my personal views as a representative, and I'm not speaking on behalf of any company. It's general discussion, not medical advice, and not advice for your situation. Please look after yourself, and I'll see you next time.
