## Script

You're tired. Not end-of-a-long-day tired. Tired in a way a weekend doesn't seem to fix. And there's something else, something you haven't said out loud. Some of the clients you used to enjoy, you've stopped caring about. You see their name in your calendar and something in you sinks.

So you wonder. Is this just a bad patch? Or is it burnout?

I want to be careful and clear from the start. I'm not a doctor, and this episode can't tell you whether you have burnout or not. Nobody can do that from a podcast. What I can do is explain how burnout is described, so you have better words for what you might be feeling. And I'll tell you where to get proper help. If you're struggling right now, please don't wait for the end of this episode. You can call National Mindline on one seven seven one, or the Samaritans of Singapore on one seven six seven. Both are open twenty-four hours. If you or someone else is in immediate danger, call nine nine five, or go to the nearest A and E.

I'm Jason Ng, and this is Quiet Compounding.

[pause]

Today we start a new theme: the working week and your energy. For the last stretch of episodes, we've talked about building a pipeline without pressure. But a pipeline is only as steady as the person running it. So for the next few weeks, we're turning the attention to you.

And we're starting with the hardest word first. Burnout.

Here's the one idea I want you to take away. Burnout is not a vague feeling, and it's not a character flaw. It has a description. And once you can see its parts, you can ask a much more useful question than "what's wrong with me?"

Let's start with the description. In May twenty nineteen, the World Health Organization included burn-out in its International Classification of Diseases, known as ICD eleven. The World Health Organization describes it as a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed.

Notice that last part. "Not been successfully managed." It doesn't say stress is the problem. Work has stress in it. Our work certainly does. The point is stress that keeps going, without enough being done about it.

And here's a detail worth knowing. The World Health Organization lists burn-out as an occupational phenomenon. It sits in a chapter called "Factors influencing health status or contact with health services". It is not classified as a medical condition. That matters. It tells you burnout is about the relationship between a person and their work. It's not a label for a weak person.

[pause]

Now the useful bit. The World Health Organization describes burn-out in three parts.

The first is exhaustion. Feeling drained, worn out, running on empty.

The second is increased mental distance from your job. Or feeling negative, even cynical, about it.

The third is reduced professional efficacy. A sense that you're not as effective as you were. That what you do doesn't make much difference.

Here's why that matters for us. If you only look for tiredness, you might miss what's going on. Tiredness alone isn't the whole picture. The early signs can show up in the second and third parts first. You dread certain client calls. You go through the motions in a review you used to enjoy. You finish a good meeting and think, "so what?" Those quieter signals are worth paying attention to.

[pause]

So where does it come from? This is where I find the research really practical.

Two researchers, Maslach and Leiter, wrote about this in a paper published in the journal World Psychiatry in twenty sixteen, they describe burnout in the same three parts: exhaustion, cynicism, and a sense of inefficacy. And they set out six areas of work life that are linked to it.

Let me give you the six. Workload. Control. Reward. Community. Fairness. And values.

Their idea is that burnout tends to build where there's a mismatch between a person and their work in one or more of those areas. Too much load. Too little say over how you work. Effort that doesn't feel recognised. Feeling alone. Something that feels unfair. Or doing work that rubs against what you believe in.

I love this framing, because it changes the question. Instead of asking "what's wrong with me?", you can ask, "which of these six areas is out of line for me right now?" That's a question you can actually work with.

It also tells you what doesn't help. If the mismatch is in values, a longer holiday won't change it. If the mismatch is in control, pushing harder won't change it either. You'd come back to the same setup and feel the same way. That's why "just push through" and "take a break and it'll go away" are not the advice I'd give. Rest matters. But rest on its own doesn't fix a setup that's out of line.

Think about it in our world for a moment. Workload might be the number of meetings and the admin behind them. Control might be how much say you have over your calendar. Reward isn't only money. It's whether the work feels recognised. Community is whether you have people around you. Fairness is whether the way things are run feels right. And values is whether the work you're doing matches why you came into this job.

And for those of us who are self-employed, there's an extra layer. You set a lot of your own workload and your own hours. That gives you real control, which is a good thing. But it also means nobody else is watching your limits. No manager is going to notice you've taken on one evening event too many. You have to notice it yourself. Or better, let someone else help you notice.

[pause]

Let me give you a picture of how this might look.

Picture an adviser who used to look forward to her annual reviews. She liked them. Catching up, seeing how a family's life had moved on. But over a few months, she starts rescheduling them. One, then another. She's still hitting her activity targets, so on paper, everything looks fine. But she feels flat. And after a meeting that went well, she catches herself thinking, "what's the point?"

She doesn't diagnose herself. She just sits down with the six areas. She goes through them one by one. Workload, honestly, is fine. Reward is fine. Community is fine. But two stand out. Control, and values. Somewhere along the way, she's said yes to a run of evening events she doesn't believe in. They're eating her evenings, and she doesn't feel they fit the way she wants to work.

So she does three things. She talks it through with her leader. She also speaks to a counsellor. Together, they help her drop two of those commitments. And she books a check-up with her GP, because feeling this flat for this long is worth a proper conversation with a doctor.

Nothing about that is dramatic. She didn't quit. She didn't push through either. She noticed, named it, and asked for help early.

[pause]

That last part matters most to me. Burnout is not a sign of weakness. And you don't have to sort it out alone. Talking to someone early, a doctor, a counsellor, a leader you trust, or a support line, is not giving up. It's what a professional does.

So here are three things to do this week.

First, take ten minutes and do the six-area check. Write the six areas down the side of a page: workload, control, reward, community, fairness, values. Next to each one, put a quick tick, a question mark, or a cross for how it feels this month. Don't overthink it. First instinct is fine.

Second, look at your crosses and pick the one that bothers you most. Just one. Then tell one person about it this week. Your leader, a peer you trust, your partner, a friend. You're not asking them to fix it. You're just making sure you're not carrying it on your own.

Third, if what you're feeling has gone on for a while, or it's getting heavier, book a time to talk to a doctor or a counsellor. You don't need to be sure it's burnout before you go. That's exactly what they're there to help you work out. And if you need someone to talk to sooner, National Mindline is on one seven seven one, and you can stay anonymous. The Samaritans of Singapore are on one seven six seven. Both are there around the clock.

[pause]

Here's the line I'd like you to carry this week: feeling burnt out is not a verdict on who you are, it's a signal about how your work is set up.

Next time, we'll stay with the working week, and look at how you shape it so your energy lasts.

Thanks for listening to Quiet Compounding. This episode was checked by my team. Look after yourself this week. I'll see you next time.
