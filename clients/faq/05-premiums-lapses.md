# Premiums and lapses, in general terms (Q54–Q65)

General information only. It does not consider your specific objectives, financial situation or needs. Grace periods, reinstatement terms and surrender values differ between policies, so always check your own policy documents or ask me before acting.

### Q54. What happens if I miss a premium payment?
Most policies give you a grace period after the due date during which you can still pay and your cover usually continues. The length of the grace period depends on the policy. If the premium is still unpaid after that, the policy may lapse, or for some policies the insurer may use the policy's cash value to keep it going for a while. You will usually receive reminders before that happens. Check your own policy documents or ask me as soon as you know a payment may be late, so we can look at your options together.

### Q55. What does it mean when a policy "lapses"?
A lapsed policy has ended because premiums were not paid. Once it lapses, you are no longer covered, so a claim for something that happens afterwards would not be paid. Depending on the policy, there may be some value left that is paid out, or nothing at all. Some lapsed policies can be reinstated within a set period, often with conditions. Letting a policy lapse is a decision best made on purpose rather than by accident. Check your own policy documents or ask me if you think a policy may have lapsed.

### Q56. My policy has lapsed. Can I get it back?
Often you can apply to reinstate it within a set period after it lapses. The insurer will usually ask you to pay the overdue premiums, sometimes with interest, and may ask fresh health questions or for a medical check. Reinstatement is not automatic, and the insurer may decline it or add conditions. Some benefits may have waiting periods again. The sooner you act, the simpler it tends to be. Check your own policy documents or ask me, and I will find out what your insurer needs.

### Q57. I am finding premiums hard to manage right now. What can I do?
Please talk to me before you stop paying anything. Depending on the policy, there may be options such as changing how often you pay, reducing the cover, using a premium holiday or the policy's value where the terms allow it, or reviewing which policies matter most to you right now. Each option has trade-offs, such as less cover or lower future values, so it is worth understanding them first. There is no judgement here; circumstances change. Check your own policy documents or ask me, and we can look at it together.

### Q58. What is a surrender value?
Some policies build up a cash value over time. If you end (surrender) such a policy early, the amount you receive is its surrender value. In the early years, it is often much lower than the total premiums you have paid, and sometimes nothing. Many protection-only policies have no surrender value at all. Surrendering also ends your cover, and getting new cover later may cost more or be harder if your health has changed. Check your own policy documents or ask me before deciding; your policy shows the guaranteed and non-guaranteed parts.

### Q59. Should I cancel an old policy and buy a new one?
That needs careful thought. Ending a policy can mean losing value, losing cover for conditions that have developed since you bought it, and facing higher premiums because you are older. New policies may also have new waiting periods and exclusions. Sometimes a change does make sense, but it should be compared fairly, in writing, with the pros and cons of keeping what you have. Please do not cancel anything before we have talked. Check your own policy documents or ask me, and we can review this together properly.

### Q60. Why are some parts of my policy "non-guaranteed"?
Some policies have values that depend on future investment returns, expenses and claims experience, such as bonuses on participating policies or the value of investment-linked policies. These can go up or down and are not guaranteed. Illustrations for participating policies use two illustrated rates, capped at 4.25% and 3.00% a year, to show a range; they are not predictions and actual returns may be higher or lower. Source: [lia.org.sg](https://www.lia.org.sg/tools-and-resources/illustrated-investment-rate-of-return-for-par-policies/). Check your own policy documents or ask me which parts of your policy are guaranteed.

### Q61. Can I change how I pay my premiums?
Usually yes. Many policies let you switch between monthly, quarterly, half-yearly and yearly payments, or change between payment methods such as GIRO, card or, where allowed, CPF or MediSave. Paying less often can sometimes cost slightly less overall, but check the actual figures. Changes may take effect from the next due date. For hospitalisation plans, the MediSave part and the cash part follow different rules (see [Q19](02-hospitalisation-medishield-ip.md)). Check your own policy documents or ask me, and I can help with the form.

### Q62. Why did my premium change?
There are a few common reasons. Some premiums are set by age band, so they go up when you move into a new band; this is common for hospitalisation plans. Some policies allow the insurer to revise premiums for all policyholders on renewal, within the policy's terms. Riders or changes you made to cover can also affect the total. Level-premium policies usually keep the same premium for the premium term. Your renewal or premium notice explains the amount. Check your own policy documents or ask me if a change looks unexpected.

### Q63. I received a letter saying my payment failed. What should I do?
Do not ignore it. Check whether your card has expired or your GIRO arrangement has changed, and make sure there were enough funds. Then pay the overdue premium as soon as you can, ideally within the grace period, so your cover continues. If you are unsure whether the letter is genuine, contact me or the insurer through the official channels you already know, rather than any link or number in an unexpected message. Check your own policy documents or ask me, and I will help you sort it out.

### Q64. Is my policy still in force?
The quickest way to check is your latest premium notice or annual statement, the insurer's official app or customer portal, or by asking me. Check that premiums are paid up to date and that the cover dates are current. If you have several policies, I can give you a simple list of what is in force, what each covers in broad terms, and when the next premium is due. Check your own policy documents or ask me; it is a good idea to confirm this once a year at your review.

### Q65. Are my policies protected if an insurer runs into trouble?
Singapore has a Policy Owners' Protection Scheme, administered by the Singapore Deposit Insurance Corporation (SDIC). Insured policies are protected up to specified limits, and cover is automatic; you do not need to apply. It is not a promise that every dollar is protected, so it is not right to say your money is 100% protected by the government. For details of the types of benefits and limits, see [sdic.org.sg](https://www.sdic.org.sg/). Check your own policy documents or ask me if you would like to know how this applies to your policies.
