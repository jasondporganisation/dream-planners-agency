# Ep 52 — Explanatory style: how you talk to yourself after a bad week

**Track:** The Resilient Advisor (RA)
**Theme:** 1 — A pipeline without pressure
**The adviser's real question:** "After a bad week I tell myself I'm useless and it'll never work. Does that inner voice actually matter?"

## Key points (in order)
1. "Explanatory style" is the habitual way you explain bad events to yourself. Psychologists look at three questions: is it about me (internal), will it last (stable), and does it affect everything (global)?
2. A pessimistic style says: "It's me, it's permanent, it ruins everything." A more optimistic style says: "Part of this was circumstance, it's this week, it's this one area."
3. This is not about positive thinking or ignoring mistakes. It's about accurate explanations that leave you able to act.
4. An often-cited 1986 study of sales agents found that those with a more optimistic explanatory style sold more and were less likely to quit. It is one study, from one sales setting, decades ago.
5. Explanatory style can shift with practice. A controlled trial with financial services sales agents found that a cognitive-behavioural training programme improved attributional style, well-being and job satisfaction, and reduced turnover.
6. A simple practice: after a bad week, write the event, your first explanation, then test it. What's the evidence? What else could explain it? What is one thing in your control?
7. If the inner voice turns to hopelessness for more than a short while, speak to someone. That's what support is for.

## Hypothetical example
Picture an adviser who has had two cancelled appointments and a client who went quiet. On Friday night his first thought is, "I'm not cut out for this. Nothing ever works for me." He tries the three questions. Is it all him? One cancellation was a sick child. Is it permanent? Last month was fine. Does it affect everything? His existing clients are happy. He rewrites it: "This week was thin, partly bad luck, and my follow-up was slow. Next week I'll confirm meetings the day before." It's still a bad week. It's no longer a verdict on his life.

## Your action this week
After your next disappointing day, write three lines: what happened; your first explanation; a more accurate one that answers "me or circumstance? this week or forever? this area or everything?". End with one action in your control.
