# Ep 48 — Pay Yourself First

**Theme:** Money habits and behaviour
**Listener's real question:** "I always mean to save what's left at the end of the month, but nothing's ever left. What am I doing wrong?"

## Key points (in order)
1. Saving "what's left" relies on willpower at the worst time, the end of the month.
2. "Pay yourself first" flips the order: save on payday, then spend what remains.
3. MoneySense suggests saving at least 20% of monthly take-home pay. Treat it as a direction, not a rule you fail.
4. Automate: a standing instruction on payday removes the decision.
5. Use separate pots (emergency, goals, everyday) so money has a job.
6. Start smaller if you need to (5–10%) and step up with each pay rise (see Ep 49).
7. CPF is already a built-in "pay yourself first" for retirement and housing. Cash savings are for the rest of life.

## Worked example (illustrative numbers only)
"Kelvin" takes home an illustrative $4,000 a month.
- Pay-yourself-first at 15%: $600 moves out automatically on payday, $400 to emergency and $200 to a travel goal.
- He lives on $3,400. After a year he has about $7,200 set aside, without "trying to save".
- When his pay rises, he lifts the transfer before his spending catches up.
*Say on air: "Illustrative. The point is the order, not the exact percentage."*

## Common myths to correct
- "I'll save once I earn more." Spending usually rises with income (Ep 49).
- "Saving 20% is impossible, so why bother?" Any automatic amount builds the habit.
- "CPF already saves for me, so I don't need cash savings." CPF is mostly locked for specific purposes.
- "Budgeting means tracking every cent." Automation does most of the work.

## "Never say" lines
- "Automatically invest your savings in [product]."
- "Anyone who can't save 20% is irresponsible."
- Any implied return figure on savings.
