# Ep 72 — How Scams Find You

**Theme:** Protecting what you build
**Listener's real question:** "I think I'd never fall for a scam, but my friend did. How do they actually work, and what protection is there?"

## Key points (in order)
1. Scams succeed because of urgency, authority and emotion, not because victims are careless.
2. Scale: reported losses were about $1.11 billion in 2024 and about $913.1 million in 2025 (Police).
3. Common types in 2025: investment scams (~$336.2m) and government-official impersonation (~$242.9m, up 60.5%).
4. Safeguards exist. The Shared Responsibility Framework (from 16 Dec 2024) requires banks to give 12-hour cooling-off periods, real-time alerts and a self-service kill switch. Telcos must filter and block certain SMS.
5. The Protection from Scams Act (in force 1 Jul 2025) lets Police issue restriction orders on bank accounts of people believed to be at risk of being scammed.
6. Personal habits matter most: pause, check through official channels, never share OTPs, consider a money lock.
7. If in doubt, call the 24/7 ScamShield Helpline 1799. If money has moved, call your bank immediately.

## Worked example (illustrative scenario)
"Uncle Ong" gets a call from someone claiming to be a government officer. They say his account is linked to a crime and he must move money to a "safe account" today.
- Red flags: urgency, authority, secrecy, and a request to transfer money.
- A calm response: hang up. Call 1799 or the agency's official number found independently. Never share OTPs.
- If he had already transferred money: call the bank's hotline at once and make a police report.
*Say on air: "A made-up scenario, based on common patterns Police describe."*

## Common myths to correct
- "Only old people get scammed." Victims span all ages.
- "Government officers might ask me to transfer money." Government officials will not ask you to transfer money over a call.
- "The bank will always refund me." The SRF sets out duties and a loss-sharing order. Refunds are not automatic.
- "If the caller knows my details, they're legit." Personal details are often leaked or bought.

## "Never say" lines
- Naming specific banks' features as better or worse.
- "You'll definitely get your money back."
- Shaming victims ("how could anyone fall for that").
- Describing scam techniques in replicable step-by-step detail.
