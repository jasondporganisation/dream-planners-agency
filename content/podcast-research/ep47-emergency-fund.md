# Ep 47 — The Rainy-Day Fund, Sized Calmly

**Theme:** Money habits and behaviour
**Listener's real question:** "How much cash should I keep for emergencies, and isn't it wasteful to leave it sitting there?"

## Key points (in order)
1. An emergency fund is not an investment. It is shock absorption: a job loss, a medical bill, a family trip at short notice.
2. MoneySense's rule of thumb is to set aside at least three to six months' worth of expenses.
3. Size it on expenses, not income. Start by finding your real monthly spending.
4. Adjust within the range: lean higher with one income, variable pay, dependants or self-employment, and lower with stable dual incomes.
5. Keep it accessible and separate from day-to-day spending, so it isn't spent by accident.
6. Build it in steps: one month first, then three, then six. Automate the transfer.
7. "Sitting there" is its job. It buys calm and stops you borrowing at high interest in a crisis (see Ep 50).

## Worked example (illustrative numbers only)
"Farah" tracks two months of spending and finds she uses an illustrative $3,200 a month.
- 3 months: $9,600. 6 months: $19,200.
- She has one income and supports her mum, so she aims for the upper half: about $16,000 to $19,000.
- She automates an illustrative $600 a month into a separate account. That takes about 2.5 years to reach $16,000, with a first milestone of $3,200 in under six months.
*Say on air: "Made-up numbers to show the method. Your own range depends on your situation."*

## Common myths to correct
- "My credit card is my emergency fund." That is debt at 25–29% a year.
- "I'll invest it instead, it's wasted in cash." An emergency fund's job is availability, not growth.
- "Six months of salary." It is six months of expenses.
- "I need the full amount before I start anything else." Build it in steps.

## "Never say" lines
- Naming specific savings accounts, banks or interest rates on deposits.
- "Put your emergency fund in [any product] for better returns."
- "Everyone needs exactly six months."
