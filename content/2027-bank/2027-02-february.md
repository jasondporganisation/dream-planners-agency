### 09 · A red packet plan before the visiting starts

**Theme:** Budgeting · **Flag:** None

**Hook:** Lunar New Year is lovely. The bank statement afterwards, less so.

**Post:**

The festive season adds up quietly: red packets, new clothes, goodies, hosting, travel between homes. None of it feels big on its own.

A simple plan before the visiting starts:
1. Write down every household you'll give red packets to, and decide the amount for each group (family, friends' children, colleagues).
2. Add the total of everything else: food, gifts, outfits, transport.
3. Take that total out of your main account into a separate "festive" pot.
4. When the pot is empty, that's your signal to pause, not to top up with your card.

Planning ahead doesn't make you less generous. It means you can give with a clear head, and start the year without a surprise bill in March.

Gong xi fa cai to everyone celebrating.

### 10 · Give your bonus a job before it arrives

**Theme:** Budgeting · **Flag:** None

**Hook:** Decide what your bonus is for before it lands, not after.

**Post:**

Once money is sitting in your account, it starts to feel like spending money. That's why bonuses so often disappear without anyone remembering where.

Try deciding in advance. Before your bonus is paid, split it on paper:
• A part for anything you owe, starting with the most expensive debt
• A part for your safety buffer, if it's not where you want it
• A part for longer-term goals
• A part to enjoy, guilt-free

The exact split depends on your situation. The point is that every part is decided by you, calmly, not by whatever shows up in your feed the week the money arrives.

Write the plan down, and when the bonus comes in, move the money the same day.

What's one thing your bonus is going towards this year?

### 11 · A "money date" for couples who are already married

**Theme:** Family protection concepts · **Flag:** None

**Hook:** Valentine's Day idea that's less romantic, but more useful: a money date.

**Post:**

Couples often talk about money a lot before the wedding, then stop once life gets busy.

A money date is a short, regular check-in. Coffee, no phones, maybe thirty minutes. Some questions to cover:
• What came in and went out this month? Any surprises?
• Are we still working towards the same goals?
• Is anything worrying either of us that we haven't said?
• If one of us couldn't work for a while, would we know what to do?
• Do we both know where the important documents are?

The aim isn't to audit each other. It's to make sure neither person is carrying all the money worries alone.

If quarterly sounds like a lot, start with one this month and see how it feels.

### 12 · Getting married cancels your will

**Theme:** Wills, LPA and nominations · **Flag:** None

**Hook:** Made a will when you were single? Getting married may have cancelled it.

**Post:**

This surprises a lot of people: in Singapore, a will is revoked when you marry or remarry.

So if you made a will before your wedding, you may effectively have no will now. Your estate could then follow the intestacy rules instead of the wishes you wrote down.

The same happens with CPF: marriage revokes an existing CPF nomination. Divorce, on the other hand, does not revoke a CPF nomination, which can catch people out the other way.

A simple rule: after any marriage, review both your will and your CPF nomination. After a divorce, review your CPF nomination too.

Know a couple who just got married? This is a kind thing to share with them.

### 13 · What a CPF nomination does not cover

**Theme:** Wills, LPA and nominations · **Flag:** None

**Hook:** You made a CPF nomination. Does that cover your flat? No.

**Post:**

A CPF nomination is useful, but it has clear limits. Knowing them helps you see what else needs planning.

A CPF nomination covers your savings in your Ordinary, Special, MediSave and Retirement Accounts, and any CPF LIFE premium balance.

It does **not** cover:
• Property bought with your CPF savings
• Payouts from the Dependants' Protection Scheme
• Investments made under the CPF Investment Scheme

Those follow other rules, such as how the property is held, the scheme's own arrangements, or your will.

So "I've done my CPF nomination" is a good start, but it isn't the whole plan. Your flat, your bank accounts and your other assets still need to be thought through.

### 14 · Deductible vs co-insurance, in plain English

**Theme:** MediShield Life and hospitalisation basics · **Flag:** Date-sensitive. Uses deductibles in force in 2026; a second phase of deductible increases takes effect 1 Apr 2027 (new amounts not yet published). Post before 1 Apr 2027 or update.

**Hook:** Two hospital-bill words most people nod along to: deductible and co-insurance.

**Post:**

Here's what they mean under MediShield Life.

**Deductible:** the first part of the bill you pay each policy year before MediShield Life starts paying. For a Class B2 stay, if you're 80 or below (age next birthday), it's $2,500. You pay the full deductible only once per policy year.

**Co-insurance:** after the deductible, you still pay a share of the claimable amount. It's 10% of the first $5,000, 5% of the next $5,000 and 3% above $10,000 (these bands include the deductible).

Why design it this way? MediShield Life is basic cover for large bills, sized for subsidised wards. It's meant to share costs with you, not remove them.

Knowing these two words makes any hospital bill easier to read.

### 15 · Describe a normal Tuesday at 65

**Theme:** Retirement habits · **Flag:** None

**Hook:** Most retirement plans start with a number. Try starting with a Tuesday.

**Post:**

Ask people about retirement and they talk about holidays. But most of retirement isn't holidays. It's ordinary days.

So try this exercise. Write a few lines about a normal Tuesday when you're 65.
• Where do you live?
• Are you working at all, part-time or for fun?
• Who do you see?
• What do you do in the morning? In the afternoon?
• What do you spend money on that day?

It sounds simple, but it turns a vague goal into something you can picture. And once you can picture it, it's much easier to talk about what it might cost and what needs to happen between now and then.

Do it with your partner and compare. The differences are often the most useful part.

### 16 · What the job actually is

**Theme:** Career and adviser life · **Flag:** None

**Hook:** "So you sell insurance?" Here's how I'd describe my work instead.

**Post:**

When people ask what I do, the short answer is that I'm a representative who helps families plan for the things they'd rather not think about.

The day-to-day is less about products and more about:
• Listening. Most first conversations are about people's worries, not their policies.
• Explaining. CPF, MediShield Life and estate planning are confusing. Making them clear is a big part of the job.
• Following up. Reviews, reminders, and being there when someone needs to make a claim.
• Staying within the rules. Everything I share publicly is checked against the rules before it goes out.

It's not for everyone. It takes patience and a lot of learning. But on the days a family tells me they finally feel sorted, it's very meaningful work.
