### 34 · A Mother's Day conversation worth having

**Theme:** Family protection concepts · **Flag:** None

**Hook:** This Mother's Day, along with flowers, ask Mum one gentle question.

**Post:**

Many of our mothers have quietly run the household paperwork for decades. Bills, policies, bank books, the drawer where everything important lives.

So here's a gentle question to ask, maybe over a long lunch: "Mum, if you ever needed help with your paperwork, where would I start?"

You're not asking about money or inheritance. You're asking how you can help her if she ever needs it.

Some things she might mention:
• Where her important documents are kept
• Whether she's made a will or an LPA
• Who her doctor is, and any medical wishes she has
• Who she'd want you to contact

Listen more than you talk. And if she isn't ready, that's okay. You've opened the door.

### 35 · Who can witness your will?

**Theme:** Wills, LPA and nominations · **Flag:** None

**Hook:** Your friend witnessed your will. Your friend is also in it. That's a problem.

**Post:**

A will in Singapore has some basic formalities. According to MoneySense:
• You must be 21 or over and of sound mind.
• You sign it in front of two witnesses aged over 21.
• The witnesses sign in your presence.
• The witnesses, and their spouses, must not be beneficiaries.

That last point catches people out. If you ask a family member who's named in your will to witness it, it creates a problem for that gift. The safest approach is to choose witnesses who receive nothing under the will and aren't married to anyone who does.

Whoever helps you prepare it, double-check your witnesses before anyone signs.

Small details, big consequences.

### 36 · A will does more than divide money

**Theme:** Wills, LPA and nominations · **Flag:** None

**Hook:** Parents with young children: a will isn't only about who gets what.

**Post:**

When people think of a will, they think of dividing assets. But MoneySense notes that a will also lets you choose:
• **Who inherits** your estate
• **A guardian** for your children under 21
• **An executor**, the person who carries out your wishes

For many young parents, the guardian decision matters more than anything else in the will. Who would raise your children? Have you asked them? Do they know?

The executor matters too. It's a real job: paperwork, accounts and dealing with institutions, often while grieving. Choose someone organised and trustworthy, and ask them first.

These are hard conversations. They're also some of the most loving ones a parent can have.

### 37 · Estate planning for Muslim families: the basics

**Theme:** Wills, LPA and nominations · **Flag:** None. Suggest Jason checks the wording with a Muslim colleague or the Syariah Court pages before posting.

**Hook:** For Muslim families in Singapore, estate rules work differently.

**Post:**

If you're Muslim, your estate in Singapore is distributed under section 112 of the Administration of Muslim Law Act. The Syariah Court issues the Inheritance Certificate.

A will still has a role. Through a will, a Muslim can give away at most one-third of the estate, to people who are not already entitled to inherit.

For CPF savings, if no nomination is made, the Public Trustee's Office distributes them according to a Muslim Inheritance Certificate.

Because the rules are specific, it's worth getting guidance from someone who knows them well, especially if your family situation is more complex.

Whatever your faith, the principle is the same: understand the rules that apply to you, then plan within them.

### 38 · Trust nomination vs revocable nomination

**Theme:** Wills, LPA and nominations · **Flag:** None

**Hook:** Two kinds of insurance nomination. They work very differently.

**Post:**

In Singapore, a life insurance policy can have a nomination that decides who receives the payout, outside your will. There are two kinds.

**Trust nomination** (Insurance Act 1966, s132)
• Takes priority over your will.
• Nominees can only be your spouse or children.
• The policy owner must be 18 or over and sign before two witnesses.

**Revocable nomination** (Insurance Act 1966, s133)
• The insurer pays according to whichever is the last known document: the revocable nomination or your will.

So if you make a revocable nomination and later write a will that says something different, the later document may decide.

Whichever you use, keep your nominations and your will consistent.

### 39 · No nomination on your policy? Here's what happens

**Theme:** Wills, LPA and nominations · **Flag:** None

**Hook:** You have life insurance, but no nomination. Who gets the money?

**Post:**

If there's no nomination on a life insurance policy, MoneySense explains that the insurer may pay up to $150,000 to a "proper claimant". Anything above that goes into the estate.

Once it's in the estate, it follows your will. If there's no will, it follows the intestacy rules.

That can mean:
• The money may take longer to reach your family.
• It may not go to the person you had in mind.
• It's handled together with the rest of the estate.

A nomination doesn't replace a will. Your other assets still need planning. But for policies, it helps the payout reach the people you intended.

Check each of your policies: is there a nomination, and is it still the one you want?

### 40 · Got a raise? Watch for lifestyle creep

**Theme:** Budgeting · **Flag:** None

**Hook:** You earn more than five years ago. So why doesn't it feel like it?

**Post:**

Lifestyle creep is when your spending quietly rises to match your income. A nicer phone, more takeaways, a bigger car, pricier holidays. Each one feels reasonable. Together, they absorb every raise.

It's not wrong to enjoy more as you earn more. The problem is when it happens without a decision.

One simple approach: when you get a raise, decide upfront how it will be shared. For example, part to your goals and part to enjoy. Then set up the transfer before the first bigger pay cheque lands.

That way, your lifestyle still grows. It just grows on purpose, and your future self gets a share too.

What's one upgrade you made that you'd actually skip if you thought twice?

### 41 · What I learnt from my first mentor

**Theme:** Career and adviser life · **Flag:** None

**Hook:** The most useful thing my first mentor taught me had nothing to do with sales.

**Post:**

When I started in this industry, I thought the job was about having the right answers.

My mentor taught me it was about asking the right questions. And then listening, properly, without rushing to a solution.

Some other things I picked up:
• Say "I don't know, let me check" instead of guessing.
• Write things down after every meeting.
• Follow up when you say you will, even with bad news.
• If something feels off, ask compliance before you do it, not after.

I try to pass these on to the newer members of our team. Mentoring isn't a favour. It's how good habits survive in an industry that changes all the time.

Who taught you something that shaped how you work?
