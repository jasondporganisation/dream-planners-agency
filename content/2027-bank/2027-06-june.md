### 42 · Father's Day: "Don't worry, I'll handle it"

**Theme:** Family protection concepts · **Flag:** None

**Hook:** Many dads say "I'll handle it." The kindest thing is to make sure someone else could, too.

**Post:**

A lot of fathers carry the family's money matters quietly. They know which bills go out when, where the documents are, who to call. Nobody else does.

That's a lot of trust. It's also a single point of failure.

This Father's Day, a small gift to your family: share the map.
• Show your partner where the key documents are kept.
• Walk through which accounts pay which bills.
• Explain what cover the family has, in plain words.
• Tell them who to call if something happens.

You can keep handling it. The difference is that if you ever can't, nobody has to start from zero.

Happy Father's Day to all the dads who quietly hold things together.

### 43 · The mid-year money check

**Theme:** Budgeting · **Flag:** None

**Hook:** Half the year is gone. How are those January money goals doing?

**Post:**

New Year goals often fade by March. June is a good time to check in, without guilt.

Five questions, fifteen minutes:
1. What were my money goals for this year?
2. Which one am I on track for?
3. Which one have I quietly dropped? Why?
4. Did anything big change: job, family, health, home?
5. What's one thing I'll do differently in the second half?

If a goal no longer fits your life, it's fine to change it. A goal you've adjusted is better than one you've abandoned.

And if you've made progress, notice it. Most money progress is slow and invisible, so it's worth celebrating when you can see it.

Save this and do it on the last weekend of June.

### 44 · Three CPF LIFE plans, side by side

**Theme:** Retirement habits · **Flag:** None

**Hook:** CPF LIFE isn't one plan. There are three, and they pay out differently.

**Post:**

CPF LIFE pays you a monthly income for life. When you join, you choose between three plans:

**Escalating Plan:** starts lower, then rises 2% a year.

**Standard Plan:** level payouts that stay the same. They aren't adjusted for inflation.

**Basic Plan:** starts low, and payouts fall once your balances drop below $60,000.

All three pay monthly for as long as you live. And if you pass away, any remaining premium balance goes to your loved ones.

There's no "right" plan for everyone. It depends on what you'd value more: a higher starting amount, or payouts that rise over time.

Worth reading CPF Board's comparison before you need to choose.

### 45 · Waiting to start CPF LIFE payouts

**Theme:** Retirement habits · **Flag:** None

**Hook:** Start CPF LIFE at 65, or wait? Here's what CPF says about waiting.

**Post:**

If you're automatically included in CPF LIFE, you can choose to start your payouts any time from 65 to 70. If you don't choose, payouts start automatically at 70.

CPF Board says payouts rise by up to 7% for each year you defer, up to 35% if you defer all the way to 70.

That doesn't mean deferring is always the better choice. It depends on things like:
• Whether you're still working
• What other savings or income you have
• Your health and family situation

It's a personal decision with real trade-offs. The useful habit is to think about it in your early 60s, not the month you turn 65.

Note: CPF LIFE payouts are estimates that depend on your plan and balances.

### 46 · Who's automatically in CPF LIFE?

**Theme:** Retirement habits · **Flag:** None

**Hook:** Will you be in CPF LIFE automatically? It depends on two things.

**Post:**

You're automatically included in CPF LIFE if you're a Singapore Citizen or PR born in 1958 or later, and you have at least $60,000 in retirement savings when your payouts start.

If you aren't auto-included, you can still join voluntarily any time from 65 up to one month before you turn 80.

Why is this worth knowing early? Because your CPF balances in your 50s and 60s shape what your retirement income could look like later. Knowing the rules helps you understand where you might land.

A good habit for anyone in their 50s: log in to your CPF account once a year and look at the retirement section, even if 65 still feels far away.

### 47 · Single? You still need a plan

**Theme:** Family protection concepts · **Flag:** None

**Hook:** "I'm single, no kids. I don't need to plan." Let's talk about that.

**Post:**

Being single changes your plan. It doesn't remove the need for one.

Some questions that matter just as much when you're on your own:
• **If you were hospitalised,** who would help you, and how would the bills be paid?
• **If you lost mental capacity,** who would make decisions for you? Without an LPA, someone would have to apply to court.
• **If you passed away,** who should receive your savings? Without a will or nominations, the law decides.
• **In retirement,** who will you rely on? For many singles, the honest answer is mostly yourself.

Planning as a single isn't about protecting dependants. It's about making sure you're looked after, by people you've chosen.

Single friends, which of these have you sorted?

### 48 · Your year-at-a-glance money calendar

**Theme:** Budgeting · **Flag:** None

**Hook:** Some months are always expensive. Do you know which ones?

**Post:**

Money stress often comes from timing, not totals. Everything seems to land in the same month.

Try making a simple money calendar for the year:
• Mark your big recurring costs by month: insurance renewals, road tax, school fees, festive spending, holidays.
• Mark your income bumps: bonus, any irregular income.
• Mark key admin dates: tax filing, policy reviews, your CPF quarterly check.

Look at it and you'll see the pressure points, like a December with gifts, holidays and renewals all at once.

Then you can plan ahead: save a bit more in quiet months, move a renewal date if possible, or set up a sinking fund.

One page. Stick it on the fridge.

### 49 · What families actually value

**Theme:** Career and adviser life · **Flag:** None

**Hook:** What do people value most from an adviser? In my experience, it's not what you'd guess.

**Post:**

When I started, I assumed people wanted the cleverest plan.

Over time, I've noticed what families really appreciate is simpler:
• **Being reachable.** Picking up the phone when something goes wrong.
• **Plain language.** Explaining things without jargon or pressure.
• **Honesty.** Saying "you don't need this" when that's the truth.
• **Remembering.** Knowing their situation without making them repeat it.
• **Patience.** Letting them take time to decide.

None of these show up on a sales chart. All of them build trust over years.

If you're thinking about this career, ask yourself whether those things come naturally to you. They matter more than any pitch.

### 50 · An adviser's own money habits

**Theme:** Career and adviser life · **Flag:** None

**Hook:** Commission income goes up and down. So I pay myself a steady salary.

**Post:**

One thing new advisers aren't always ready for: income that isn't the same every month.

Some months are good. Some are quiet. If you spend based on your strongest month, the quiet ones hurt.

What helped me:
• **Pay yourself a fixed amount** each month from a holding account, based on a cautious estimate, not your strongest month.
• **Keep a bigger buffer** than someone on a fixed salary might need.
• **Set aside money for tax** as income comes in, not when the bill arrives.
• **Don't upgrade your lifestyle** on one strong quarter.

It's the same advice I'd give anyone with irregular income: freelancers, sales staff, business owners.

Stability isn't about how much you earn. It's about how you manage what comes in.
